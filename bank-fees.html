Your Bank Charges You $200 a Year for Your Own Money

October 2026

Jasmine is inspired by a real person — names and details changed so the story can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real 2026 data or a clearly labelled assumption.

Jasmine is 24, works as a barista in Saskatoon, and does food delivery on weekends. She rents a two-bedroom with two roommates. She is, by her own admission, not a person who looks at bank statements. Statements are for people who enjoy bad news.

One night she looked anyway. And there it was, one quiet line, every single month: $17.95.

Not for an overdraft. Not for using another bank's machine. Not for anything she did wrong. Just for having the account. The bank charges her to hold her own money.

This article is about that line.

The fee, stated plainly

A chequing account is a place to put your paycheque so you can spend it. That's the whole job: money goes in, money goes out. The big banks charge a monthly fee for that job, and the standard unlimited-transaction accounts cluster around the same price.

Here are the fees as of October 2026, from the banks' own pages:

Account Monthly fee A year's worth
TD Unlimited Chequing $17.95 $215.40
BMO Performance Chequing $17.95 $215.40
RBC Signature No Limit Banking $16.95 $203.40

The title of this article says $200. That is rounded down, and you should know it: the exact number for the two $17.95 accounts is $215.40 a year. Not $200. Two hundred fifteen dollars and forty cents, every year, for the privilege of accessing your own paycheque.

It is easy to miss because $17.95 feels small. It is designed to feel small. It arrives once a month, buried between your coffee purchases and your e-transfers, and your brain files it under "cost of being an adult." But multiply it out and it stops feeling small.

The ten-year version

If the fee never changed — and fees do change, so treat this as an illustration, not a prediction — ten years of $17.95 a month is $2,154.

That is $2,154 for nothing you can hold, nothing that grows, nothing that comes back. It is not an investment. It is not savings. It is a toll booth on your own money, and you drive through it every month.

Now, is $2,154 over ten years life-changing? No. Nobody goes broke on chequing fees. That is exactly why the fee works: it is small enough to ignore and permanent enough to add up. The irritation in this article's title is the right reaction. You are not being overcharged in a dramatic way. You are being charged, quietly, forever, for something that costs the bank very little to provide.

The minimum-balance trap

Here is the part that makes it worse. Both TD and BMO will waive that $17.95 fee — if you keep $4,000 in the account at all times. TD's rule is $4,000 or more at the end of each day of the month. BMO's is a $4,000 daily minimum balance.

Read that rule again, slowly. At the end of each day. Not your average balance. Not "usually around $4,000." Every single day. Dip to $3,999 on one Tuesday because rent came out before your paycheque landed, and you pay the full $17.95 that month.

And the $4,000 itself is the second way you pay. That money sits in a chequing account, earning no interest, doing nothing. It cannot go into your TFSA. It cannot pay down a debt. It cannot be invested. It is frozen in place, guarding a fee waiver.

To see what that freeze costs, take a clearly labelled assumption: suppose that $4,000 could otherwise earn 4% a year. That is $160 a year in growth you are giving up — not a promise, not a rate anyone guarantees, just the arithmetic of what parked money does not do. So your two options are:

What you pay What it costs you
Pay the fee $17.95 a month $215.40 a year, gone
Park $4,000 to waive it $0 a month $4,000 frozen — plus, on a 4% assumption, $160 a year in growth you never see

Either way the account costs you. The only difference is whether the cost shows up as a line on your statement or as money that never grows.

This is the trap, and it is aimed precisely at people like Jasmine: people whose balance swings. Paycheque in, rent out, groceries, gas, a slow delivery weekend — the balance moves. A $4,000 floor is easy for someone with a steady salary and a cushion. It is a tightrope for someone whose money arrives in pieces.

The third option nobody mentions at the branch

There is a third option, and it is the reason this article exists: chequing accounts with no monthly fee exist. Online banks offer chequing with a $0 monthly fee and unlimited transactions. The basic job — money in, money out — is available for nothing.

This article does not recommend any particular one, and there are no links here. The point is simpler: the job itself is not worth $215.40 a year. Holding your money and moving it around is a solved problem, and the price of the solved version is zero.

So what does the $17.95 actually buy? Honestly, mostly this: branches, tellers, and a building with your bank's name on it. If you walk into a branch regularly, talk to a person, and value that, then the fee buys something real and the choice is yours. Scenario honesty cuts both ways.

But if you bank the way Jasmine does — on your phone, at midnight, after a delivery shift — then you are paying $215.40 a year for branches you never visit. That is the question worth asking yourself, and it is a one-line question: when was the last time I actually went into my bank?

Two things worth checking before anything else

First, several banks waive fees entirely for students and young adults, usually 24 and under. If that is you, you may already be eligible for a $0 account at the bank you have — no switch required. Check before you assume you are stuck.

Second, switching takes some admin. Your direct deposits and pre-authorized payments live at your old account, and moving them takes an afternoon of forms. That is friction, not a fee — but it is real, and it is the main reason people stay. Knowing that is useful: the bank is not keeping you with a better deal. It is keeping you with inertia.

The verdict

Your bank charges you $200 a year for your own money. Actually $215.40, if we are being exact — the title rounds down, and you deserve the real number.

The fee is not a scam. It is a price, printed on the bank's own page, for a job that exists elsewhere for $0. The minimum-balance waiver is not a favour. It is a second price, paid in frozen money instead of a monthly line.

Jasmine has not decided anything yet. She is still doing the math — which is the point. Open your own statement, find your own $17.95 line, multiply it by twelve, and ask what it buys you. If the answer is branches you never visit, you already know what to do next.
