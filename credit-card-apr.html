Your Credit Card Is a 21% Loan

October 2026

Jasmine is inspired by a real person — names and details changed so the story can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real 2026 data.

Jasmine is 24, works as a barista, and does food delivery on weekends. She rents a two-bedroom with two roommates in Saskatoon. She is smart and funny and deeply allergic to lectures.

Last spring she bought herself a new laptop, some clothes, and concert tickets — about $3,000 on her credit card. Nothing crazy. She told herself she'd pay it off "soon."

Every month, she paid the minimum. Every month, her statement said the balance was still around $3,000. Month after month after month. It felt like she was pouring money into a hole.

So she asked Priya — her brother Rob's wife, the household spreadsheet keeper — a question she was embarrassed to ask out loud: "Is my credit card broken? I keep paying and it never goes down."

Priya looked at the statement for about ten seconds and said: "It's not broken. You're paying a 21% loan. That's how loans at 21% behave."

Your card is a loan. Just a badly disguised one.

Most people never hear it said that way. A credit card feels like money. It isn't. It's a loan you can draw from again and again.

And it is one of the most expensive loans you will ever carry. Typical Canadian credit card rates run 19.99% to 20.99% APR. Read that again. A mortgage is around 5%. A student loan is around 6 or 7%. A credit card is 21%. It is the penthouse of borrowing costs.

But there's a magic trick built into the card, and if you use it, the loan never happens.

The 21-day grace period: the magic trick

Here's the one rule that changes everything.

Every month your card gives you a grace period — usually 21 days — to pay your statement balance. Pay the full statement balance by the due date, and you pay zero interest. Not 21% interest. Zero. You get to borrow the money for up to 51 days (statement period plus grace period) for free.

Miss it by even one dollar and the trick collapses. Pay $1 less than the full balance, and interest gets charged from the purchase date — not the due date, not the end of the grace period, the day you bought the thing. The free ride was never real if you don't pay in full.

So there are exactly two ways to use a credit card:

  1. Pay it in full every month. The card is free short-term credit. This is a payment tool.
  2. Carry a balance. The card is now a 21% loan. This is a loan.

Most people drift from option 1 to option 2 without noticing, because of the minimum payment.

Minimum payments are designed to keep you in debt

Look at any credit card statement. There's a small box showing the "minimum payment." Usually it's the greater of $10 or about 3% of the balance. It looks reasonable. Helpful, even.

It's not. It's the slowest possible escape plan, and the math is brutal.

Run the numbers on Jasmine's $3,000 balance at 20.99%, paying only the minimum (the greater of $10 or 3% of the balance) every single month:

Month 1: The card charges one month of interest: $3,000 × 20.99% ÷ 12 = $52.48. The balance becomes $3,052.48. The minimum payment is 3% of that = $91.57. After the payment, the balance is $2,960.90. Eleven of your dollars knocked only $39 off the balance. The rest was interest.

Every month looks like that. Because the minimum shrinks as the balance shrinks, you're always paying just a little more than the interest. The balance barely moves.

Final bill: 218 months. That's 18.2 years — Jasmine will be 42. Total interest paid: about $3,750.

Read that once more. She borrowed $3,000 and paid $3,750 in interest — more than the original $3,000 she spent. The bank more than doubled its money on a $3,000 laptop, some clothes, and concert tickets.

Now compare: if Jasmine had paid just $100 a month instead of the minimum, she'd clear the whole thing in 43 months (under 4 years) and pay about $1,290 in interest. Same debt. Same card. The minimum payment nearly tripled the interest and added fourteen years.

The minimum payment is not a plan. It's a leash.

The rule

Priya's rule for Jasmine, and for everyone:

The card is a payment tool, not a loan. If you can't pay it in full this month, you can't afford it.

That's the whole article in one sentence. One idea. Use the card, collect the points, pay it in full on the due date, pay zero interest. The moment you carry a balance, you're borrowing at 21% — the most expensive loan most people will ever sign for.

Jasmine hated the lecture. She paid off the card in five months instead. She still has it. She just treats the statement balance like a due date on a library book now — full payment, on time, no exceptions.

What about the points and perks?

Honest answer: points are a discount on a tool, not a reason to carry debt. If you pay in full every month, 1–2% cashback is genuinely free money. But no rewards program on earth pays 20.99%. A $3,000 balance earning 2% cashback while charging 21% interest is losing you about $570 a year. Never keep a balance to chase points. Ever.

The trap inside the trap

One more thing the statements don't shout: when you carry a balance even once, new purchases lose the grace period too. Until the whole balance is paid in full, every new charge starts collecting 21% interest from day one. That one month you didn't pay in full keeps taxing every purchase you make afterward. This is why getting back to zero matters — it's the only way to reset the free ride.
