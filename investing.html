Investing, Without the Jargon

October 2026

Jasmine is inspired by a real person — names and details changed so the story can be told honestly. Every number in this article is a clearly labelled illustration — an assumption, not a promise.

"Just buy stocks"

Jasmine was wiping down the espresso machine when her coworker said it.

"You should just buy stocks," he said, like it was obvious. Like everyone knew what that meant.

Jasmine nodded. She had no idea what it meant.

Buy stocks of what? From where? What even is a stock — a piece of paper? A number on a screen? She'd heard the word a thousand times and understood it zero times.

That night she asked Priya — Rob's wife, the one with the spreadsheet and zero patience for jargon. "Explain investing to me," Jasmine said. "Like I'm five."

Priya smiled. "Okay. Three building blocks. Then three rules. That's the whole thing."

This article is Priya's explanation.

Building block 1: a stock is a slice of a company

A company is worth some amount of money. A stock is a tiny slice of it.

Buy one slice of a coffee chain, and you own a crumb of every café, every espresso machine, every bag of beans. If the company does well, your slice becomes worth more. If it does badly, your slice becomes worth less. You can sell your slice to someone else whenever you want.

That's it. A stock is not magic. It's a slice.

The catch: one slice of one company is a wild ride. The company has a bad year, your slice drops 30%. The company has a great year, it jumps 40%. One slice, one company, all your eggs in one basket.

Building block 2: a bond is a loan you give

Flip it around. Instead of owning a slice, you lend money.

A government or a big company says: "Lend us $1,000. We'll pay you back in ten years, plus a little extra each year for the trouble."

That "little extra" is interest. It's calmer than stocks. Your money grows slowly and steadily. But it grows slowly — that's the trade. Bonds rarely jump. They also rarely crash.

Think of it this way: stocks are the roller coaster, bonds are the merry-go-round. Different rides. Different thrills. Different risks.

Building block 3: a fund is a basket

Here's the fix for the one-slice problem.

A fund is a basket that holds slices of hundreds of companies at once. You buy one basket, and you instantly own a crumb of every company inside it.

There are two main kinds of baskets, and the difference barely matters for a beginner:

  • A mutual fund is a basket managed by a person who picks the slices.
  • An ETF is a basket that usually just copies a list — like "the 500 biggest companies" — with no person picking.

Same idea, different wrapper. One purchase, hundreds of slices, instant spread.

This is the single most important building block in the article. Remember the basket.

The one paragraph on risk and return

Everything in investing is a trade between calm and growth. The calm options — a savings account, a bond — grow slowly and rarely fall. The wild options — stocks — can grow faster over long stretches, but they can also drop hard, and stay down for years. More possible reward always comes with more possible pain. They are welded together. Anyone who offers you big rewards with no risk is either confused or lying to you.

Rule 1: diversify (own the basket, not the slice)

Remember the coworker's advice? "Just buy stocks." Which stocks? He didn't say. That's the problem.

If you buy one company's stock and that company sinks, your money sinks with it. Ask anyone who owned one hot company in 2000. But if you own a basket of hundreds of companies and one sinks, the basket barely wobbles. The other 499 carry it.

Diversification is just the old advice with better math: don't put all your eggs in one basket. So buy the basket with many baskets inside. One fund, hundreds of companies, done.

This fits IF you want investing to be boring and automatic. Skip picking single stocks IF you can't explain what the company does in one sentence — because then you're not investing, you're guessing.

Rule 2: keep fees low (the quiet eater)

This is the rule nobody tells beginners, and it's the one that costs them the most.

Every fund charges a yearly fee — a small percentage of your money, taken quietly, every year, whether the fund goes up or down.

Watch what "small" does over time. Assumption, labelled: two baskets both grow at 6% a year before fees. You put $10,000 in each and leave it for 30 years.

  • Basket A charges 2% a year. You keep 4%. Final amount: $32,434.
  • Basket B charges 0.2% a year. You keep 5.8%. Final amount: $54,271.

Same growth. Same 30 years. The only difference is the fee — and it ate $21,837.

Read that again. A fee that sounds tiny — less than two percentage points apart — swallowed more than a third of the money. Fees compound just like growth does, except they compound against you.

Fees are the one part of investing you fully control. You can't control the market. You can control what you pay to ride it. So pay as little as possible.

Rule 3: think long-term (and expect the bumps)

The basket will drop. Not "might." Will.

Some year, the news will be terrible, your basket will fall 20%, and everyone will tell you to sell. Every long-term investor in history has lived through this. The ones who did well are the ones who didn't panic — because they had put in money they wouldn't need for years, and they left it alone.

Time is what turns the wild ride into something calmer. Year to year, stocks bounce violently. Decade to decade, the bouncing has historically smoothed out — historically, which is not a promise about your decade. Nobody can promise returns. Anyone who does is selling something.

This fits IF you won't need the money for many years and a bad year won't make you sell in a panic. Keep it small or skip it IF you need the money soon — investing money you'll need next year isn't brave, it's a coin flip.

The bottom line

Jasmine stared at Priya's napkin sketch: a slice, a loan, a basket.

"That's it?" she said. "That's the whole thing?"

"That's the whole thing," Priya said. "A stock is a slice of a company. A bond is a loan you give. A fund is a basket of slices. Buy the basket. Pay tiny fees. Leave it alone for years. Everything else is noise."

Jasmine's coworker meant well. But "just buy stocks" skips every part that matters — which stocks, at what fee, for how long. Now she knows the parts. The jargon is gone. What's left is three building blocks and three rules, and they fit on a napkin.
