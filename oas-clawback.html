The OAS Clawback: The Line That Shrinks Your Pension

October 2026

Nadia is inspired by a real person — names and details changed so the story can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real 2026 data.

Nadia is 58. She's a school division administrator in Saskatoon, married, two grown kids. She did everything the way you're supposed to: steady job, condo nearly paid off, RRSPs in most years.

And lately she's been doing math at the kitchen table. The anxious kind. Out loud.

"Seven years to retirement," she told her husband last week, pushing a stack of papers across the table. "I've been counting on the government pension. Then my sister-in-law told me the government can take part of it back if you make too much. Is that real? What line? What counts?"

It is real. It has a name nobody likes: the OAS recovery tax. Everyone calls it the clawback.

Here's how it actually works — no jargon, no panic.

The one sentence that explains everything

Once your income crosses a line the government sets each year, the government takes back 15 cents of your OAS pension for every dollar you earn above that line.

That's it. That's the whole mechanism. There's no penalty, no fine, no drama. It's a sliding scale: the more you earn over the line, the more OAS you repay. It shows up on your tax return, not as someone knocking on your door.

For the 2026 income year — which decides your OAS payments from July 2027 to June 2028 — the line is $95,323 of individual net income.

Under the line? Your full OAS. Over it? The 15-cents-per-dollar math kicks in. At around $154,700 (for ages 65 to 74), the OAS is fully clawed back — every dollar repaid.

One line. Fifteen cents a dollar. Those two facts are the entire system.

The three things people get wrong

Nadia's anxious math was mostly wrong about the details. These are the three myths her kitchen-table worry session ran into — and what the truth is.

Myth 1: "They'll look at our household income."

No. The line is per person.

The CRA tests your income against your line. Your spouse has their own separate line. If Nadia earns $120,000 and her husband earns $55,000, his OAS is completely untouched — the clawback only looks at Nadia's number.

This is the single biggest misunderstanding. Couples often panic that one higher earner ruins both pensions. It doesn't. Each person's OAS stands or falls on their own income alone.

Myth 2: "Pulling money from my TFSA will push me over the line."

No. TFSA withdrawals don't count.

The "net income" the clawback uses is line 23600 on your tax return. TFSA withdrawals never land on your tax return at all — you already paid tax on that money before you put it in. Pull $30,000 from a TFSA in retirement, and your clawback math doesn't move a single dollar.

This matters because it makes the TFSA a quiet hero in retirement: it's income you can use that the clawback can't see.

Myth 3: "If one of us is over the line, we're stuck."

Not necessarily. Pension income splitting can fix it.

If one spouse receives eligible pension income — like a workplace pension or RRIF payments after 65 — they can split up to 50% of it with the other spouse on their tax returns. (No money actually moves; it's a paper allocation.)

Picture this: Nadia's husband has a workplace pension paying $100,000 a year. Nadia has no pension income. His income is $100,000 — over the $95,323 line, so some OAS gets clawed back.

But he can split half the pension with Nadia. Now he shows $50,000, she shows $50,000. Both are miles under the line. Both keep their full OAS.

Pension splitting can't move every kind of income, and you don't have to split exactly half — the point is to shift just enough to pull the higher earner under the line. A tax preparer can run the exact split in minutes.

The worked example: Nadia's number

Let's run Nadia's actual worry through the math.

Say at 65, Nadia's income — pension, RRSP/RRIF withdrawals, CPP, OAS, everything that lands on line 23600 — totals $110,000 for the year.

Step 1: How far over the line?

$110,000 − $95,323 = $14,677 over.

Step 2: Multiply by 15 cents on the dollar.

$14,677 × 0.15 = $2,202.

That's it. For the year, Nadia repays about $2,202 of her OAS — roughly $183 a month. Not her whole pension. Not a punishment. A partial reduction, proportional to how far she is over the line.

Now notice what she could do with Myth 3. If she can shift income to her husband through pension splitting — or draw a little more from her TFSA (Myth 2: invisible to the clawback) instead of her RRIF that year — she can slide herself back under the line and keep the whole $2,202.

That's why this article exists. The clawback isn't something that happens to you. Once you know where the line is and what counts, you can plan around it.

The line is real, but it's not a wall

Here's what Nadia took away from the kitchen table, and it's what matters for anyone doing the same anxious math:

  • Know the line. $95,323 for the 2026 income year. It gets adjusted every year, so check it again when retirement is closer — but the idea never changes: 15 cents back per dollar over.
  • Know what counts. Net income on line 23600 of your tax return. Not your household. Not your TFSA withdrawals.
  • Know your tools. Pension splitting can move taxable income between spouses on paper. TFSA withdrawals give you spending money the clawback can't see.

The clawback sounds scary because the word "clawback" sounds like something being ripped away. In practice it's arithmetic — gentle, gradual arithmetic. Fifteen cents at a time.

And here's the part nobody at Nadia's kitchen table had said out loud: being over the line means you have a good income in retirement. That's the problem having a good income. The math shrinks your pension a little — it never takes it all, and it never should keep you up at night.
