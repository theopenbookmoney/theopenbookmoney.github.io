Take CPP at 60 or Wait Until 70? The $700-a-Month Question

October 2026

Elena and Frank are inspired by real people — names and details changed so their stories can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real 2026 data or a clearly labelled assumption.

The question over Sunday dinner

Rob is 38. Retirement is decades away. But last Sunday, watching his parents clear the table, he asked the question anyway.

"When do I take CPP? Sixty? Sixty-five? Seventy?"

Frank, 70, answered fast. "Sixty. I took mine the month I could. Best decision I ever made."

Elena, 67, shook her head. "I waited until sixty-five. And I wish I'd waited longer."

Same household. Same CPP. Two opposite answers — and both of them are sure they're right.

This article is the math behind their argument. It's one of the biggest money decisions a Canadian makes, and most people make it with a shrug. Don't shrug. Here's how it works.

The rules, in plain words

CPP pays you a monthly cheque for the rest of your life. The standard start age is 65. But you can start any time between 60 and 70. And the age you pick changes the cheque — permanently.

Start before 65: your cheque shrinks by 0.6% for every month early. That's 7.2% a year. Start at 60 — five years early — and your cheque is 36% smaller. Forever.

Start after 65: your cheque grows by 0.7% for every month you wait. That's 8.4% a year. Wait until 70 and your cheque is 42% bigger. Forever.

Past 70: nothing more. The increase stops at 70, so waiting longer just leaves money on the table.

Three things to tattoo on your brain:

  1. The adjustment is permanent. Take it at 60 and the 36% haircut never goes away. Wait to 70 and the 42% bonus never goes away.
  2. You don't have to stop working. You can collect CPP and keep earning. Starting early is not retirement — it's just turning on the tap.
  3. The cheque is indexed to inflation and paid until you die. A bigger cheque at 70 is also a bigger cheque at 85 and 95. That matters more than most people think.

What the timing does to real money

Let's make it concrete. Labelled assumption: your CPP at 65 would be $1,000 a month. (Yours will differ — check your estimate in My Service Canada Account. Most people get less than the maximum. The timing math works the same on any amount.)

Start age Adjustment Monthly cheque
60 −36% $640
65 none $1,000
70 +42% $1,420

Read that spread: $640 versus $1,420 a month — a $780 monthly gap, for life — decided by nothing but timing.

For context, the real 2026 numbers from the Government of Canada: the maximum CPP at 65 is $1,507.65 a month, and the average new person starting at 65 gets about $858 a month (July 2026 figures). Few people get the maximum. Whatever your number is, the −36% / +42% adjustments scale it the same way.

Frank took his at 60. His cheque is smaller than Elena's every single month, and it always will be. He knew that going in. "I wanted the money while I could still enjoy it," he says. Elena waited. Her cheque is bigger every single month. "Mine has to last longer," she says. "I'm the worrier."

The break-even: when the math flips

Here's the question everyone really asks: at what age does the patient person pull ahead?

Think of it this way. The person who starts at 60 collects money for five years while the waiter collects nothing. That's a head start. But after 65, the waiter's bigger cheque starts clawing it back, month by month. The break-even age is when the claw-back catches up.

On our $1,000-a-month assumption:

  • 60 vs 65: the early starter banks $38,400 in those five years ($640 × 60 months). After 65, the 65-starter gains $360 a month. $38,400 ÷ $360 = about 107 months — the break-even is around age 74. Live past 74, and starting at 65 pays more in total.
  • 65 vs 70: the waiter gives up five years of $1,000 cheques ($60,000) to gain $420 a month. $60,000 ÷ $420 = about 143 months — the break-even is around age 82. Live past 82, and waiting to 70 wins.

Simple version: if you live a long time, waiting wins. If you don't, starting early wins. The break-even ages — mid-70s and early-80s — are the fulcrum the whole decision balances on.

One honest caveat: this is simple math, not a promise. It ignores inflation indexing subtleties, taxes, and the fact that money today can be invested. But as a rough guide, those two ages — 74 and 82 — are the numbers to hold in your head.

When starting early is right

Take it at 60 IF:

  • Your health is poor, or your family history says the odds of a long life aren't great. The break-even at 74 only matters if you get there.
  • You need the money now. Debts to clear, bills to pay, no other income to bridge the gap. A smaller cheque today beats a bigger cheque you can't afford to wait for.
  • You're still working and want the income. Plenty of people take CPP at 60 while working full-time. The cheque is just extra cash flow. (Frank did exactly this — railway pension plus CPP at 60, while he was still picking up shifts.)

When waiting wins

Wait until 65 or 70 IF:

  • You're healthy with longevity in the family. If your parents lived into their late 80s, the odds favour you collecting the bigger cheque for decades past the break-even.
  • You can afford the wait. Other savings, a work pension, or part-time income that covers the gap years. Waiting is a luxury — it costs five years of cheques.
  • You want the cheapest longevity insurance in Canada. A 42%-bigger, inflation-indexed cheque paid until death is hard to beat. No insurance company sells anything this good at this price, because no insurance company has to.

What Elena and Frank would tell their 30-year-old selves

Frank: "I don't regret 60. I got the money when I was young enough to use it. But do the math first — I did, and it still worked."

Elena: "Find out your real number. Don't assume the maximum. Service Canada shows your estimate in ten minutes, and everything in this article scales off that number."

Both of them: "It's permanent. You get one shot. So give it more than a shrug."

The bottom line

CPP timing is a bet on your own lifespan, and the terms are printed on the tin: −36% at 60, +42% at 70, break-evens around 74 and 82.

If you'll live long and can afford to wait, waiting is the best deal in Canadian retirement finance. If your health or your bank account says otherwise, take it early and don't apologize — that's what the early option is for.

Elena's summary, taped to the fridge next to all the other notes: "Sixty is $640. Seventy is $1,420. The only wrong answer is not knowing those two numbers."
