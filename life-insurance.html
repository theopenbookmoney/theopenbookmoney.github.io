Life Insurance: The One You Need vs the One They Sell You

October 2026

Rob and Priya are inspired by real people — names and details changed so their stories can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real 2026 data or a clearly labelled assumption.

The pitch at the kitchen table

Rob and Priya have two kids, a mortgage, and a minivan. Maya is 8. Liam is 5.

Last month an insurance agent sat at their kitchen table with a glossy folder. He talked for an hour. His message was simple: "Term insurance is renting. Whole life is owning. Why would you rent protection when you can own it?"

The number at the bottom of the folder: $320 a month. For life.

Rob almost signed. It sounded responsible. Permanent. Grown-up. Priya said the sentence that saved them $3,480 a year: "Let me check the math first."

This article is the math.

What life insurance is actually for

One sentence. Life insurance exists to replace your income if you die and someone depends on it.

That's the whole job. Not investing. Not saving. Not building wealth. Replacing income for the people who would be broke without it.

And here is the part the glossy folder skips: for most families, the need is temporary. It is biggest right now — young kids, big mortgage, small savings — and it shrinks every year. The mortgage gets paid down. The kids grow up and earn their own money. The savings grow. By 60, most families don't need life insurance at all, because there's nobody left depending on the paycheque.

Keep that in mind. It decides everything.

Term life: pure insurance

Term life is the simple one. You pay a fixed amount every month for a set period — 10, 20, or 30 years. If you die during that period, your family gets the payout, tax-free. If you outlive it, the policy ends and you get nothing back.

Read that again, because people hear it as a flaw: you get nothing back. That's not a flaw. That's the point.

Term insurance is like car insurance. You pay every year. If you don't crash, you don't get a refund — and you don't feel cheated. You paid for protection during the years you needed it. Term life works exactly the same way: you pay to protect the years your family is most vulnerable.

And it's cheap, because that's all it does. Illustrative premiums, clearly labelled — your actual quote will differ by age, health, and insurer: a healthy 35-year-old non-smoker might pay around $30 a month for $500,000 of 20-year term coverage. Twenty years of protection for about $7,200 total.

Whole life: insurance bundled with an investment account

Whole life is the complicated one. It covers you for your entire life — the payout comes whenever you die. But part of every premium goes into a cash value account inside the policy, which grows tax-deferred at a modest rate, usually around 2 to 4% a year.

Sounds nice. Here's the price: that same 35-year-old might pay around $320 a month for the same $500,000 of coverage. That's more than ten times the term premium. Over 30 years, that's about $115,200 in premiums versus $7,200 for term.

What do you get for the extra $108,000? Lifetime coverage you probably won't need (remember: the need shrinks), plus a cash value account growing at a rate your TFSA would beat with one hand tied behind its back.

And two catches the folder doesn't highlight:

  • The cash value takes years to build. Cancel in the first several years and you can walk away with far less than you paid in. Surrender charges can apply for a decade or more.
  • When you die, the insurer typically keeps the cash value and pays your family only the face amount. You spent decades building a savings account your family never sees.

The honest comparison

Same person. Same $500,000 of coverage. Labelled illustrations:

Term (20-year) Whole life
Monthly premium ~$30 ~$320
Total paid (30 yrs) ~$7,200 (20 yrs) ~$115,200
Cash value at the end $0 ~$140,000–$180,000
Payout if you die at 81 $0 (expired) $500,000

Now the question the agent never asks: what if you bought term and invested the $290-a-month difference yourself?

$290 a month for 30 years at a labelled illustrative 6% average return grows to about $290,000 — in your own account, under your control, withdrawable any time. Compare that to the whole life cash value of roughly $140,000 to $180,000. Same monthly outlay. Very different ending.

(The catch, stated honestly: this only works if you actually invest the difference, every month, for 30 years, and leave it alone. The forced savings of whole life helps people who know they'd spend the difference instead. Know yourself.)

Why the agent pushed the expensive one

Now the part that explains the kitchen-table pitch. Insurance agents earn commission as a percentage of the premium. Whole life commissions commonly run 50% to 100% of the first-year premium. Term commissions run roughly 30% to 80% — of a far smaller premium.

Do the labelled illustration: on that $320-a-month whole life policy, the agent can take home roughly $1,900 to $3,800 in year one alone. On the $30-a-month term policy, the same agent earns roughly $110 to $290.

Same customer. Same kitchen table. Ten to twenty times the paycheque — for the product that costs the family ten times more.

This isn't a conspiracy theory. It's an incentive structure, and it's public information. It doesn't mean every agent is dishonest. It means you should know what the person across the table earns from each option before you let them tell you which one you need.

Who actually needs what

Scenario honesty, the way this site works.

You need term life insurance IF:

  • Someone depends on your income. Kids, a spouse who earns less, a mortgage that needs two incomes. That's Rob and Priya — textbook case.
  • Your need has an end date. Kids independent by 60, mortgage gone by 60 — a 20- or 25-year term covers the vulnerable years.

You probably don't need much IF:

  • You're single with no dependents and no co-signed debts. Nobody is depending on your paycheque, so there's no income to replace.

Whole life fits IF — and it's a short list:

  • You have a lifelong dependent, like a child with special needs who will need support after you're gone.
  • You're wealthy enough to be planning your estate — maxed-out TFSA and RRSP, looking for a guaranteed tax-free legacy.
  • You know, honestly, that you'd spend the "invest the difference" money instead of investing it.

That's it. For everyone else, the bundle is the wrong product at ten times the price.

The pitches to recognize

The agent at Rob and Priya's table used three classics. You'll hear them too:

"Term is renting, whole life is owning." A house can go up in value. Insurance is a cost either way — the question is how much cost, for how long.

"With term, your premiums are wasted." Your car insurance premiums are "wasted" every year you don't crash. You still buy it. Premiums buy protection, not a refund.

"It's tax-free." The death benefit is tax-free under both term and whole life. That's not a reason to pick one over the other.

The bottom line

Priya ran the numbers, and Rob didn't sign the folder. They bought a 20-year term policy for a fraction of the price and put the difference toward their actual investments — where they control it.

The rule fits on a sticky note, next to the emergency-fund note on their fridge: buy insurance for insurance, and invest for investing. Don't buy an expensive bundle of both from someone whose paycheque depends on you saying yes.

Insurance is for the years your family can't afford to lose you. Those years end. Buy the product that ends with them — and keep the $290 a month.
