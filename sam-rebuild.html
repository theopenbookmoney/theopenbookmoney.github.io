Starting Over at 45: Sam's Money Rebuild Checklist

October 2026

Sam is inspired by a real person — names and details changed so the story can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real 2026 data.

Sam didn't plan to rebuild. Nobody plans it. At 43, his divorce settlement split everything down the middle — house sold, pension divided, bank accounts halved — and he walked into a rented townhouse with half a retirement fund, a lot of paperwork, and one recurring thought: I am 43 and starting over.

He's 45 now. The rebuild is working. And when a coworker at the warehouse started going through his own split, Sam sent him a text that was basically a checklist. No pep talks. No vision boards. Just the order he wishes he'd had on day one.

Here it is — five steps, in order, no fluff.

1. Update your beneficiaries EVERYWHERE

This is first because it's the one with a time bomb in it. Your RRSP, your TFSA, your life insurance policy, and your workplace pension plan each have a beneficiary named on them — and that beneficiary designation overrides your will. If your ex is still named on your RRSP, your RRSP goes to your ex, even if your brand-new will says otherwise. This is the classic forgotten landmine of every divorce: people update the will and forget the account forms. So pull out every account you own — bank RRSP, employer plan, TFSA, any insurance — and check who each one names. The first step is embarrassingly boring: call your bank and your HR department and ask for the beneficiary forms. Fifteen minutes of paperwork beats a five-figure surprise.

2. Rebuild the budget for one income

A two-income budget doesn't survive the split. Rent, groceries, insurance, the car — every shared cost lands on one paycheque now, and most people discover their old budget was quietly depending on the other salary covering the gaps. So you build a new one from zero, not from the old one. List what's actually coming in (your paycheque, any child or spousal support), list what's actually going out (every bill, every subscription, every coffee), and find the gap before the gap finds you. The honest truth from Sam: he thought he knew his spending. He did not know his spending. Run the numbers on our free budget calculator — it takes a few minutes and shows you exactly where the money goes, so you can fix the leak instead of guessing. A budget after a split isn't about deprivation; it's about knowing the real number you're standing on.

3. Rebuild the emergency fund from zero

The old emergency fund probably got split along with everything else, and what Sam had left covered roughly three weeks. Here's the thing people get wrong: they wait until they can rebuild the full three-to-six months before starting, which means they never start. The checklist says start with $500. Not $5,000 — $500. A starter cushion covers the car repair, the vet bill, the furnace filter emergency that used to be "we'll figure it out" and is now "me, alone, figuring it out." Put it in a separate savings account so you don't accidentally spend it, and let the first deposit be small enough that it actually happens this week. A half-finished emergency fund beats a perfect plan that never left your head. Once $500 is sitting there, grow it month by month until it covers three to six months of your one-income expenses — but don't let the perfect target block the first step.

4. Rebuild your credit, patiently

Divorce doesn't directly wreck your credit score — but the mess around it can. Joint accounts left open with an ex, a missed payment during the chaos months, suddenly carrying balances solo on one income: all of it can show up on your credit report, and lenders read that report when you rent, finance, or refinance. The rebuild is boring and it works. Step one: get a secured credit card (you put down a deposit, the deposit is your limit) and put one small recurring bill on it. Step two: pay it in full, on time, every single month — no exceptions, no "I'll catch up next month." Step three: wait. Scores in Canada run from 300 to 900, and the biggest ingredient is a long, clean history of on-time payments. There is no shortcut, no service that fixes it fast, and anyone selling one is lying. Small limits, automatic payments, patience. The score recovers. It just doesn't recover on your schedule.

5. Retitle the retirement plan

This is the hard one, and it's last on purpose — you can't think straight about retirement until the day-to-day is under control. Your old retirement picture was built for two people pooling everything. That picture is gone. The new question is simpler and harder: what does enough look like for one person, on one income, with the years you have left? Sam's version of this was ugly arithmetic: he lost roughly a decade of savings in the split, so he ran the numbers at his current contribution rate and found out exactly how far behind the old plan he was. Facing it beat dreading it, because once you see the real number you can actually act on it — raise contributions a little now (the 2026 TFSA limit is $7,000 a year, and it grows tax-free, which is a lot more than most people realize), push the retirement date a bit later, or trim the target lifestyle. None of those options are fun. All of them are better than the alternative Sam almost chose, which was not looking. You're 45, not 25, and that's genuinely an advantage: you know what you spend, you know what you're worth, and you have enough working years left to close the gap — if you start this year instead of next.

The rebuild is a checklist, not a comeback story

Sam hates comeback stories — the kind where the divorced guy gets rich and buys a boat. That's not this. His rebuild was paperwork, a spreadsheet, a secured credit card with a $500 limit, and a retirement number he didn't want to look at. Two years in, he can look at it. Nothing exploded, nothing got rich quick, nothing was inspirational. It just got done, one item at a time.

If you're starting over, start at the top of the list. Fifteen minutes on the beneficiary forms. Then the budget. Then $500. Small, boring, finished.
