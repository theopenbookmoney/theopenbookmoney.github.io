How Much Do You Need to Retire?

October 2026

Elena and Frank are fictional — their story is invented to make the math stick. Every number in this article is real 2026 data or a clearly labelled assumption you can change in the calculator below.

The question at the kitchen table

Elena is 67. Frank is 70. They live in the same Saskatoon bungalow they bought in 1994. Frank retired from the railway with a modest pension. Elena worked retail and saved what she could.

Last Sunday, Rob asked them the question everyone is afraid to ask out loud: "How did you know you had enough?"

Frank laughed. "We didn't. We guessed."

Elena was more honest. "We got lucky. Frank's pension covers a lot. But if I could talk to my 30-year-old self, I'd tell her one thing: figure out your number early. We never had a number. We just hoped."

This article is the number Elena wishes she'd had.

The gap: the only number that matters

Forget "how much do I need to retire?" for a moment. Start with a better question: how much of your spending will your savings have to cover?

In Canada, part of your retirement is already handled. Two programs do the heavy lifting:

  • CPP (Canada Pension Plan). You and your employer paid into it every working year. In 2026, the maximum is $1,507.65 a month at 65 — but most people get far less. The average new person starting CPP at 65 gets about $858 a month, or roughly $10,300 a year.
  • OAS (Old Age Security). This one is not based on work. It is based on how long you lived in Canada. In late 2026, the maximum is $762.50 a month from 65 to 74, rising to $838.75 a month at 75.

Add them up and a typical Canadian couple might get $25,000 to $35,000 a year from these two programs alone — before touching a dollar of savings.

So the real question is: after CPP and OAS (and any work pension), how big is the yearly gap? That gap is what your savings must fill. Everything in this article is about that gap.

The 25x rule

Here is the simple version. A financial planner named William Bengen studied every 30-year stretch of US market history starting in 1926. He asked: how much could a retiree take out each year without running dry?

His answer, published in 1994: about 4% of the starting savings per year. That is the famous "4% rule."

Flip it around and you get the rule of 25: multiply your yearly gap by 25. That is roughly what you need saved.

Why does it work? If you need $35,000 a year from savings, and you take 4% a year, then $35,000 ÷ 0.04 = $875,000. Take 4% of $875,000 in year one and you get exactly $35,000.

Where the rule wobbles

Bengen's rule is a rule of thumb, not a promise. Here is what it assumes — and where real life can differ:

  • It assumes a 30-year retirement. Retire at 65 and live to 95, and the math gets tighter.
  • It assumes roughly half stocks, half bonds. All cash? The math breaks. All crypto? Also breaks.
  • It is based on US history. Canada is similar but not identical.
  • It ignores a bad start. If the market crashes in your first years of retirement, the rule gets shaky. This is the biggest real risk, and no rule of thumb fixes it.
  • It ignores taxes and fees. You will pay both.

The rule is a starting point, not a finish line. It tells you the size of the mountain. It does not climb it for you.

The worked example: Rob's number

Rob is 38. Let's give him a number, the way Elena wishes someone had given her one. All assumptions are labelled.

Assumption Value
Yearly spending needed in retirement $60,000
Pension income (CPP + OAS estimate) $25,000
Yearly gap savings must fill $35,000
Retirement number (25 × gap) $875,000

Read that again. Rob does not need to save "as much as possible" — a goal nobody can picture. He needs roughly $875,000 in today's dollars, on top of CPP and OAS.

Now, is he on track? Say Rob has $150,000 saved today and puts away $1,000 a month for the next 20 years, earning an average of 6% a year (an assumption, not a promise):

  • The $150,000 grows to about $497,000
  • The $1,000 a month grows to about $462,000
  • Total at 65: about $959,000

That is roughly $84,000 more than his $875,000 number. On these assumptions, Rob is on track — barely. Change the return to 5%, or the monthly amount to $800, and the answer flips. That is why the calculator below matters more than this example: your numbers are not Rob's.

What Elena and Frank would tell their 30-year-old selves

Elena: "Get your number. Not 'save more' — a number. $875,000 is scary, but scary you can plan for beats vague you can't."

Frank: "And check it every few years. Life moves. The number moves with it."

Elena: "Also — find out what your CPP will actually be. Don't assume the maximum. Most people don't get it. Service Canada will show you your estimate. It takes ten minutes and it changes everything."
