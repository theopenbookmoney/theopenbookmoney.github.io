The Bank's Mortgage Insurance Isn't Life Insurance

October 2026

Rob and Priya are fictional — their story is invented to make the math stick. Every number in this article is real 2026 data.

The day Rob and Priya signed their mortgage papers, the bank made them an offer. It sounded warm and responsible. "Mortgage life insurance," the advisor said. "If anything happens to one of you, the mortgage is taken care of. Your family keeps the house."

Rob almost said yes on the spot. It sounded like life insurance, and life insurance sounded like a thing responsible parents have.

Then Priya asked the question that changed everything: "Who gets the money?"

The advisor blinked. Then she answered honestly — and they said no.

Here's the thing the name hides: the bank's "mortgage life insurance" is not life insurance. It's creditor insurance — insurance on your debt, sold to you, paying the creditor. Everything that makes it a bad deal flows from that one fact.

Who gets the money: the bank, not your family

This is the whole game, so let's make it concrete.

Imagine Rob and Priya owe $400,000 on their mortgage. Rob dies. Two different policies, two very different outcomes.

Bank creditor insurance. The payout goes to the bank. The bank uses it to pay off whatever is left on the mortgage. Priya keeps the house — but she gets no money. Not a dollar. The groceries, the kids' school costs, the income Rob's paycheque used to cover — none of that is the policy's problem. The family it "protects" gets a roof and an empty bank account.

Real term life insurance. The payout goes to Priya — she's the beneficiary, the person named on the policy. She gets a tax-free lump sum, say $500,000, and she decides what happens. She can pay off the mortgage if she wants. Or keep the mortgage and use the money to live on. Or both. Her choice.

Same tragedy. Same premiums paid for years. In one version your family is protected. In the other, the bank is protected and your family is told that's the same thing.

It isn't. One policy answers the question "what does my family need?" The other answers "what does the bank need?"

Your coverage shrinks — but your bill doesn't

Here's the second trick, and it works while you're not looking.

With bank creditor insurance, the coverage equals your mortgage balance. That balance shrinks every month as you pay it down. So every month, your insurance covers a little less.

Your premium, though? It stays the same. It's set when you sign up and it does not drop as your balance drops.

Read that again. You pay the same money every month for less and less protection. Year 1, you're paying $80 a month to insure $400,000. Year 15, you're still paying $80 a month — but there's far less mortgage left to insure. Year 24, you're paying full price to insure almost nothing.

Term life does the opposite. You buy $500,000 of coverage and it's $500,000 in year 1, year 10, and year 20. Flat. The price is locked. What you pay for is what you get, every year of the term.

Paying the same price for shrinking coverage is the worst kind of deal: you can watch it happen on paper and still feel like you can't do anything about it. (You can — more on that below.)

You can't take it with you

Bank creditor insurance is glued to two things: your mortgage and your lender. It dies with them.

If Priya and Rob switch banks at renewal to chase a better rate, their bank insurance ends. If they want coverage again, they start over — re-applying at their new, older age, with their new, worse-or-better health, at a higher price. And here's the cruel part: if one of them has developed a health problem since, they can be declined. The insurance they counted on can vanish exactly when they need it most.

Term life doesn't care who holds your mortgage. It's yours. You can switch lenders five times, move across the country, change jobs — the policy follows you, and the price you locked in at 38 stays the price. Your insurance is attached to your life, not to your loan paperwork.

That portability gap gets worse with age, because every re-application happens at an older age, when insurance is more expensive and health is less certain.

It costs more per dollar — for most healthy people

This one surprised Rob the most, because the bank's version felt cheaper. It wasn't.

The numbers, for Rob's $400,000 mortgage:

Bank creditor insurance: banks price this per $1,000 of mortgage balance. A published Canadian bank rate is around $0.20 per $1,000 per month — which makes Rob's coverage $80 a month. For coverage that shrinks every year. Over 20 years, that's $19,200 in premiums for a policy that ends up covering almost nothing.

Term life: published September 2026 quotes put $500,000 of 20-year term at about $30 a month for a healthy 35-year-old non-smoking man. Flat $500,000, flat price, for the full 20 years.

Line them up: $80 a month for shrinking coverage that starts at $400,000, versus about $30 a month for a flat $500,000 that never shrinks.

Per dollar of coverage, the bank's version costs about $0.20 per $1,000 per month while term costs about $0.06 per $1,000 per month. Roughly three times the price per dollar — for an inferior product.

Why does the bank charge more? Partly convenience: it's sold at the signing table, where you're exhausted and just want the paperwork to end. And partly because healthy people subsidize sick people. The bank doesn't check your health much when you sign up — everyone pays roughly the same rate, which means the healthy 38-year-old non-smoker pays the same price as the 50-year-old smoker. Real life insurers charge healthy people less. Guess which table you'd rather sit at.

One more thing the brochure skips

This is the part Priya found in the fine print, and it's the ugliest part.

The bank asks you almost no health questions when you sign up. Sounds easy. But many bank policies do the real health check after a claim is filed — when you're dead and can't answer back. If they find a health issue you didn't disclose, they can deny the claim. The family gets nothing, and there's no do-over.

Real term life insurance does the health check before you pay a single premium. You answer the questions, you get approved, and from that day the contract is the contract. When your family files a claim, the insurer already knows your health history. That's what you're paying for: certainty that the cheque arrives.

Post-claim underwriting means the bank's policy is a promise with a trapdoor. Underwritten-upfront means the promise is solid.

The one honest exception

Now the fair part, because there is one, and honesty is the whole point of this series.

If a health condition means no real life insurer will cover you — if you've been declined for term insurance, or you know you can't pass a medical — then the bank's version may be your only option. It asks almost no medical questions, and guaranteed coverage you can actually get beats perfect coverage you can't.

That's a genuinely different situation with a genuinely different answer. A bad deal beats no deal.

But that's the exception. If you're a healthy person who could pass underwriting, you are not the exception. You're the person the bank's product is built to profit from.

Call it what it is

The bank isn't evil for selling this. They have a loan to protect, and insurance on the loan protects them. That's their job.

The problem is only the name. "Mortgage life insurance" sounds like it protects your life. It protects your mortgage. So let's rename it to what it is: mortgage debt insurance.

And the decision rule that falls out of it is just as simple: insure your life, not your loan. If you want your family to be okay without you, buy a policy whose beneficiary is your family — flat coverage, a locked price, portable to anywhere, underwritten before you pay, at roughly a third of the per-dollar price.

Rob and Priya said no at the signing table. Priya spent one evening reading actual term quotes instead. They got more coverage, for less money, that their family actually receives. That's not a trick. That's just reading the words on the page.
