Your Landlord Can’t Raise Rent Like That

October 2026

Jasmine is inspired by a real person — names and details changed so the story can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real 2026 data.

Jasmine is 24, works as a barista, and rents a one-bedroom in Ontario for $1,400 a month.

Last week she found a letter from her landlord taped to her door. Her rent was going up 5%. That's $70 more a month — $840 a year.

Her stomach dropped. She did the mental math on what $840 buys: groceries for a month, her transit pass, the credit card minimum she keeps dodging. She texted her landlord to ask why.

He replied: "Costs are up. Everything's going up."

She almost believed him. Almost.

The myth, stated plainly

The myth goes like this: your landlord owns the building, so they can charge whatever they want. If they say rent is going up 5%, 10%, whatever — that's the end of the conversation.

Most renters believe this. And landlords count on that.

It's wrong. In most of Canada, the law sets a maximum your landlord can raise your rent. It's not a suggestion. It's a ceiling. And your landlord knows exactly where it is — he was hoping you didn't.

The ceiling: what the law actually allows

Each province sets its own limit. Here they are, plain and simple:

Province 2026 limit Notice your landlord must give
Ontario 2.1% (guideline) 90 days, in writing, on a Form N1
British Columbia 2.3% 3 months' notice
Manitoba 1.8% (guideline) 3 months' written notice
Nova Scotia 5% (temporary cap, until December 31, 2027) 4 months' written notice
Quebec No fixed percentage — increases must be "just and reasonable" Tenant has one month to refuse

That's it. That's the whole picture. Your landlord can go below the ceiling. They cannot go above it.

Ontario, up close (since that's where Jasmine lives)

Jasmine's landlord said 5%. Ontario's legal maximum in 2026 is 2.1%. That means his number is more than double what the law allows.

Three more Ontario rules that matter:

  • 90 days' written notice, on a Form N1. A text message or a note taped to your door doesn't count. The notice has to be on the official Form N1 and given at least 90 days before the increase takes effect.
  • At most once every 12 months. Your landlord cannot raise your rent twice in one year, even if each raise is small.
  • One big exception: units first occupied after November 15, 2018 are exempt from the guideline. If Jasmine's building was first lived in after that date, the 2.1% cap doesn't apply. Most older apartments are covered.

British Columbia, Manitoba, Nova Scotia, Quebec

  • B.C.: the cap for 2026 is 2.3%, and it drops to 2.2% for 2027. Your landlord must give 3 months' notice.
  • Manitoba: the 2026 guideline is 1.8%. That's the number.
  • Nova Scotia: the cap is 5%, but it's temporary — it ends December 31, 2027.
  • Quebec: there's no fixed percentage. The rule is that increases must be "just and reasonable." If your landlord sends you a notice, you have one month to refuse it. You don't lose your apartment for saying no.

The worked example: Jasmine's letter

Let's do the math on Jasmine's actual situation. She pays $1,400 a month in Ontario. Her landlord announced a 5% increase.

  • What the landlord asked for: $1,400 × 5% = $70 more per month → new rent $1,470
  • What the law allows: $1,400 × 2.1% = $29.40 more per month → new rent $1,429.40

The difference: $40.60 a month. That's $487 a year the landlord asked for on top of what's legal.

Here's the sentence that ended Jasmine's stomach-drop: the legal maximum on her rent is $29.40, not $70. One law, one percentage, one multiplication — and the landlord's number falls apart.

And notice how the 5% was chosen. It's under the Nova Scotia cap. It sounds reasonable — "only 5%." But Jasmine doesn't live under Nova Scotia's rule. She lives under Ontario's. The only percentage that matters is your province's.

What to do when the letter arrives

You don't have to argue. You don't have to be rude. You just have to know the number.

Step one: check your province's cap above. Step two: do the multiplication — current rent times the percentage. Step three: if the landlord's number is higher than the legal one, say so, in writing.

If your landlord insists anyway, or if the notice didn't come on the right form with the right notice period, that's what your province's tenancy board is for. The law is on your side — the ceiling exists precisely because landlords were once allowed to charge whatever they wanted, and it didn't go well.

The verdict: check the number, then decide

Most renters get a rent-increase letter, feel the panic, and pay. The landlord's entire strategy depends on you not doing the math.
