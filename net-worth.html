Net Worth: The One Number That Actually Matters

October 2026

Sam is inspired by a real person — names and details changed so the story can be told honestly. Every number in this article is a clearly labelled example you can change in the calculator below.

Sam is 45. Two years ago his divorce split everything down the middle. He walked away with half a retirement fund and a rented townhouse in Saskatoon.

Last month his brother-in-law asked him the question everyone asks: "So what are you making now?"

Sam gave the number. His brother-in-law nodded like it meant something.

But on the drive home, Sam thought: my salary is fine. So why do I feel broke? He called Priya — Rob's wife, the one with the spreadsheet — and asked a better question.

"Forget what I earn," he said. "What do I actually have?"

Priya smiled. "Now you're asking the right question. There's a number for that. It's called your net worth."

The one number

Your net worth is simple. It is everything you own, minus everything you owe.

That is the whole formula:

Net worth = what you own − what you owe

Money people call "what you own" your assets. They call "what you owe" your debts. Same thing, fancier words. From here on, we'll just say "own" and "owe."

One number. It tells you where you really stand. Not your salary. Not your credit score. This.

Why salary lies to you

Two people earn $80,000 a year. Same pay. Same city.

The first one spends all of it. Nice truck. Big TV. Zero savings. Credit card balance that never quite dies.

The second one saves $10,000 a year and invests it. Drives an older car. Boring, on purpose.

Same salary. Completely different lives, ten years later. The first person has nothing to show for a decade of $80,000 paycheques. The second person has over $100,000 plus growth.

Salary tells you what flows in. Net worth tells you what stays. That is why salary lies. It measures the river, not the lake.

Sam's salary recovered fine after the divorce. His net worth told the real story: half a retirement fund, starting over at 43. The paycheque looked okay. The number did not.

Why your credit score isn't it either

People mix these up. They are not the same thing.

Your credit score answers one question: will the bank get its money back if it lends to you? That is all it measures. Pay your bills on time and it goes up. Miss payments and it drops.

Your net worth answers a different question: are you actually getting richer?

You can have a perfect credit score and zero savings. Plenty of people do — they pay every bill on time and keep nothing. The bank loves them. Their future self does not.

Credit score is the bank's number. Net worth is your number.

How to find yours in 20 minutes

You need two lists and one subtraction. Grab a coffee. This takes 20 minutes, once.

List 1: What you own. Write down what each thing would sell for today, not what you paid for it:

  • Money in bank accounts (chequing, savings)
  • Investments (TFSA, RRSP, non-registered accounts)
  • Your home, at what it would sell for today — be honest, not hopeful
  • Your car, at what it would sell for today
  • Anything else big enough to matter

List 2: What you owe. Every debt, full balance:

  • Mortgage (the full amount left, not the monthly payment)
  • Car loan
  • Student loans
  • Credit card balances
  • Line of credit
  • Anything else you owe anyone

Now subtract. List 1 minus List 2. That is your net worth.

Three honest notes:

  • Guess low on what you own, and count every debt. People always do the opposite. Your car is worth less than you think. Your credit card counts even if you "always pay it off."
  • Your home counts, but carefully. It is real wealth. But you still need a place to live, so it is not spending money the way savings are.
  • A negative number is information, not a verdict. It just means you owe more than you own right now. Lots of young people and lots of restarters — Sam included, not long ago — start there. The number tells you where you are. What matters is where it goes next.

Sam's numbers, worked out

Priya sat Sam down with his two lists. Everything below is an example — Sam's shape of life, round numbers, so you can see how the math works.

What Sam owns:
Half the old retirement fund (RRSP) $85,000
TFSA $12,000
Emergency savings $6,000
Car (what it would sell for) $14,000
Total owned $117,000

What Sam owes:
Car loan $8,000
Credit card balance $3,000
Total owed $11,000

Sam's net worth: $117,000 − $11,000 = $106,000.

Read that number the way Priya taught him to: it is not a grade. It is a starting line. Two years ago, right after the split, his number was barely half this. The direction is the whole game.

The only habit that matters: track it monthly

Here is the part most people skip, and it is the part that actually works.

Pick one day a month — the 1st, payday, whatever you will remember. On that day, redo your two lists and write down the number. Same method every time. Then watch the line over months and years.

The direction matters more than the number. A line going up means you are winning, even if the number itself feels small. A line going down means something needs attention, even if the number still looks big.

Two rules for tracking:

  • Don't check daily. Investments bounce up and down every day. That noise will drive you crazy and teach you nothing. Monthly is plenty.
  • Don't compare your number to someone else's. A 24-year-old renter and a 60-year-old homeowner are playing different games. Compare your number to your number from six months ago. That is the only comparison that means anything.

Sam tracks his on the 1st. Some months it barely moves. Some months the market dips and it drops a few thousand and he has to remind himself the line still points up. "It's the first number I ever had that told the truth," he says. "The paycheque never did."

What "healthy" looks like at each age

Nobody can tell you the exact number you should have. Anyone who tries is selling something. But the shape of a healthy net worth over a lifetime looks roughly like this. For context, the median Canadian family net worth was about $520,000 in 2023, according to Statistics Canada's Survey of Financial Security — though most of that is housing, not cash in the bank:

  • Your 20s: Just get above zero and stay there. Kill high-interest debt. Build a small emergency fund. The number will be small. That is normal.
  • Your 30s: Positive and growing. Debts shrinking, savings growing. If you own a home, the mortgage balance falling counts just as much as investments rising.
  • Your 40s: The number should start climbing faster. Debts mostly gone except maybe the mortgage. Retirement savings doing heavy lifting.
  • Your 50s to early 60s: Peak years. This is usually the highest the number ever gets.
  • 60s and beyond: Spending it down in retirement is normal. That is what the number was for. A falling net worth at 75 is not failure — it is the plan working.

Sam is 45 and rebuilding. His number is lower than the "healthy" shape says it should be. He knows. But his line points up every single month, and that is what he controls. "I can't get the ten years back," he says. "I can make the next ten count."

The traps

A few ways people fool themselves with this number. Sam has tried most of them.

  • Counting the house as spending money. Your home is wealth, but you cannot spend it without selling it or borrowing against it — and you still need somewhere to live.
  • Forgetting a debt. The car loan counts. The line of credit counts. The "I'll pay it off next month" card counts. Every dollar you owe is in List 2.
  • Checking too often. Monthly. Not daily. Daily numbers are weather; monthly numbers are climate.
  • Chasing someone else's number. Your brother-in-law's net worth is none of your business. Your trend line is.

The bottom line

Salary is what comes in. Credit score is what the bank thinks of you. Net worth is what you actually have.

It takes 20 minutes to calculate and one minute a month to track. And it is the only number that answers Sam's real question — not "what am I making?" but "what do I actually have, and is it growing?"
