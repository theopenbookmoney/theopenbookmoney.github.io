Your Credit Score, Decoded

October 2026

Sam is inspired by a real person — names and details changed so the story can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real 2026 data.

The number Sam was afraid to look at

Sam is 45. Two years ago, his divorce split everything down the middle. He walked away with half a retirement fund and a rented townhouse in Saskatoon.

During the split, money was chaos. Bills lived in two houses. A joint credit card sat unpaid for three months because neither of them thought it was their job anymore. The bank noticed. So did Sam's credit score.

He found out the hard way. He applied for a car loan last spring. The dealer smiled, tapped his screen, and said: "Your score is a bit low for our best rate."

Sam came to Priya — Rob's wife, the one with the spreadsheet — and asked the question he should have asked two years earlier: "What is this number, actually? And how do I fix it?"

This article is her answer.

What the number is

A credit score is a three-digit number between 300 and 900. That's it. One number, built from your history of borrowing and repaying.

Two companies in Canada build the number: Equifax Canada and TransUnion Canada. Banks, landlords, and lenders send them your payment records. The bureaus turn those records into a score. Lenders check the score when you apply for a card, a loan, or a mortgage.

Important: the bureaus don't publish the exact formula. Nobody knows precisely how many points a late payment costs. But the five ingredients are public knowledge, and so is their rough order. Here's what goes in.

The five ingredients

1. Payment history — about 35%. Did you pay on time? This is the biggest one, by far. Every on-time payment helps. Every missed one hurts. A single payment 30 days late can knock a good score down hard — and it stays on your record for years. Sam's three unpaid months on the joint card? That's what the dealer saw.

2. How much you use — about 30%. This is called utilization, and it's simpler than it sounds. If your card limit is $2,000 and your balance is $800, you're using 40%. Lenders get nervous when the number is high. Under 30% is good. Under 10% is better. You can spend $2,000 a month on a card and still have low utilization — as long as you pay most of it off before the balance gets reported.

3. How long you've had credit — about 15%. Old accounts help. An old card you've kept open for ten years tells lenders you're stable. This is why time matters: you can't rush this one. Sam's oldest card survived the divorce. That's the account doing the most quiet work for him right now.

4. New credit — about 10%. Every time you apply for credit, the lender takes a hard look at your file. That look dings your score a few points. One application is nothing. Five in a month makes you look desperate. Sam learned to stop saying yes to every store card at the checkout.

5. Your mix — about 10%. Different kinds of credit count: a credit card, a car loan, a mortgage. Managing more than one type well is a small plus. This is the smallest ingredient. Nobody should take out a loan just for the mix. That would be like buying a boat to improve your swimming.

What actually moves the number

Up:

  • Pay every bill on time. Not most bills. Every bill. This is the whole game.
  • Keep balances low. Pay cards down, ideally in full each month.
  • Keep old accounts open. Even the card you barely use.
  • Give it time. Months of steady behaviour beat any trick.

Down:

  • Missing payments. The fastest way to lose points.
  • Maxing out cards. High balances scream risk.
  • Applying for credit everywhere. Space applications out.
  • Collections, judgments, bankruptcy. The big red flags. They linger for years.

Sam's rebuild was boring on purpose: one card, paid in full every month, old accounts left open, no new applications. Eighteen months later, his score had climbed back into the "good" range. No tricks. Just the ingredients, handled in order.

Where the bands are

There's no official rulebook, but the common ranges look roughly like this:

Score Band
760–900 Excellent
725–759 Very good
660–724 Good
560–659 Fair
300–559 Poor

Don't worship the bands. Different lenders draw their own lines — that's the point of the last myth below.

The myths, busted

Myth 1: "Checking your own score hurts it."

Wrong. Checking your own score is a soft check. Soft checks are invisible to lenders and cost you zero points. You can check daily if you want. The hard check — the one that dings you — only happens when you apply for credit and a lender pulls your file to decide. The bureaus, your bank's app, and free services like Borrowell and Credit Karma all use soft checks. Looking is free. Always.

Myth 2: "Closing an old card helps your score."

Usually the opposite. Closing a card does two bad things at once. First, you lose that card's limit — so your total available credit shrinks, and any balances you carry suddenly use a bigger slice. Your utilization jumps overnight. Second, you lose the card's age, which shortens your average history. Sam almost closed his oldest card out of spite. Priya stopped him. "Pay it off, put one small bill on it a month, and leave it open," she said. "It's doing more for you asleep than most of your accounts do awake."

Myth 3: "You need a high income for a high score."

Nope. Income is not in the formula. Not your salary, not your job title, not your net worth. A minimum-wage earner who pays every bill on time beats a high earner who misses payments — every time. The score measures one thing: do you pay back what you borrow, on time? That's it. That's the whole test.

Myth 4: "There's one magic number every lender wants."

There isn't. Every lender sets its own bar, and the bar moves with the product. A mortgage lender's "good" is different from a store card's "good." Your Equifax score and your TransUnion score usually differ by 10 to 40 points anyway, because lenders report to the two bureaus on different schedules. So chasing one perfect number is chasing a ghost. Aim for the direction — up — not a digit.

The bottom line

Your credit score is not a report card on your life. It's a report card on five habits: pay on time, keep balances low, keep old accounts open, don't apply for credit like it's a hobby, and give it time.

Sam's score took a hit because life got messy, not because he was reckless. The fix was just as unglamorous: boring payments, made on time, month after month. The number climbed back because the habits changed first.

Check your own score whenever you want — it costs you nothing and hurts you nothing. Then work the ingredients in order. That's the whole secret, and it was never a secret.
