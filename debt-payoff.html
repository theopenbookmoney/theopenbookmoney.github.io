“Pay Off All Debt Before Investing” — The Rule That’s Half Wrong

October 2026

Sam is inspired by a real person — names and details changed so the story can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real 2026 data or a clearly labelled assumption you can change in the calculator below.

Sam is 45, divorced, and rebuilding. The settlement split everything down the middle — the house, the savings, the retirement fund he'd spent twenty years building. He walked away with half of all of it and a lease on a townhouse in Saskatoon.

The year of the split was expensive in the way divorces are: lawyers, damage deposits, furnishing a second household from scratch, months where the math just didn't close. Some of it went on a credit card. He now owes $8,000 on it at 20% interest — squarely in the typical Canadian range of 19.99% to 23.99%, and below the 21.34% national average Statistics Canada reported for June 2026.

But here's the thing: the bleeding has stopped. Sam's budget finally balances, and he has $500 a month freed up. Now he's stuck on the question that will decide how his next twenty years go: does he kill the $8,000 card first, or start investing to make up for the decade he lost?

Everyone has an answer, and they're all confident. His brother-in-law: "Not one dollar into investments until every debt is zero. That's the rule." A bestselling personal finance book on his nightstand says the same thing in bolder type. But Sam did his own rough math: he's 45 with half a retirement fund. Every year he waits to invest is a year of compounding he never gets back.

So he asked Priya — Rob's wife, the household's spreadsheet keeper — the question nobody else would answer with numbers: "Is the rule right? Show me."

She did. And the rule is half right, which is what makes it dangerous.

The myth, stated plainly

The myth goes like this: pay off ALL debt before you invest a single dollar. No exceptions, no nuance. Debt is an emergency; investing while you owe money is like filling a bathtub with the drain open.

It feels true because half of it is true. A 21% credit card balance is an emergency, and the people preaching this rule are picturing that card. But the rule doesn't say "pay off high-interest debt first." It says all debt. And that one word is where the damage happens — because a 21% credit card and a 4.3% mortgage are not the same animal, and treating them the same costs real money.

The reframe: the only question that matters

Forget "debt versus investing." That's the wrong race. The right question is: what's the interest rate?

Here's the insight the blanket rule misses: paying down debt earns you a guaranteed, after-tax return equal to the debt's interest rate. Every dollar Sam puts toward his 20% card "earns" 20% — guaranteed, with zero volatility — and the savings land in after-tax dollars, because the interest he avoids would have been paid with after-tax dollars. No investment on earth offers a guaranteed 20%.

So the decision is never "debt or investing?" in the abstract. It's always: is the guaranteed return of killing this specific debt better than the uncertain return of investing instead? Ask it that way, and the blanket rule collapses — because the answer depends entirely on the rate.

The math that breaks it

Line up the two ends of the spectrum:

  • Credit card at 20%: paying it down is a guaranteed 20% after-tax return. To justify investing instead, you'd need an investment that guarantees 20% after tax. That product does not exist and never has.
  • Mortgage at 4.5%: paying it down is a guaranteed 4.5% after-tax return. To justify investing instead, you'd need to beat 4.5% — and a diversified portfolio might do that over a decade. Might. The word "might" is doing enormous work in that sentence, which is exactly why the blanket rule feels safe. But "might beat 4.5%" is a legitimate, defensible bet in a way "might beat 20%" is not.

The myth treats both of these as the same decision. They are not even close to the same decision.

The worked example: Sam's credit card — kill it, no contest

Priya ran Sam's actual numbers: $8,000 at 20%, with $500 a month to deploy.

Path 1 — kill the debt first. All $500 goes to the card. It's dead in 19 months, costing $1,383 in total interest. Sam then redirects the $500 a month into investments at an illustrative 6% annual return for the remaining 41 months of a five-year window.

Path 2 — pay minimums, invest the rest. Sam pays roughly 3%-of-balance minimums on the card and invests what's left of the $500 at the same illustrative 6%.

Path 1: kill debt first Path 2: minimums + invest
After 5 years $0 debt, $22,690 invested $3,575 debt, $23,078 invested
Net worth +$22,690 +$19,503

Read that twice. In Path 2, the investment account looks bigger — money went in earlier, so it compounded longer. But the balance sheet doesn't lie: kill-first wins by about $3,200, even while granting investing the full illustrative 6%.

And the path the blanket rule is really warning about — minimums only, investing the whole $500? On 3% minimums, that $8,000 card takes 23.8 years to die and costs $9,741 in interest. On an $8,000 balance. That's the minimum-payment trap, and it's why the "kill high-rate debt" half of the rule exists.

The verdict on 20% debt is unanimous: the math, the behaviour, and the sleep-at-night factor all point the same way. Kill it. No contest.

The worked example: Rob's mortgage — investing is defensible

Now the other end. Rob's mortgage is at 4.5% — a typical rate for a Canadian renewal in October 2026, when the best advertised 5-year fixed rates sat around 4.3%. He comes into a $10,000 lump sum — a bonus, say. Prepay the mortgage, or invest it?

Prepay the mortgage: a guaranteed 4.5% annual return. Over ten years, that $10,000 prepayment saves $5,530 in interest he will never pay. Contractual. Certain.

Invest it: at an illustrative 6% annual return, $10,000 grows by $7,908 over ten years — $2,378 more than prepaying. If the 6% materializes. But 6% is an assumption, not a promise: if returns run at 4% instead, the gain is only $4,802 — less than the guaranteed mortgage saving.

This is the honest version of the debate the blanket rule refuses to have. Investing the difference is mathematically defensible here — the expected value genuinely favours it — but the edge is modest, the return is not guaranteed, and the mortgage saving is. Reasonable people can land on either side, which is precisely why a blanket rule is the wrong tool. (One nudge toward investing: inside an RRSP or TFSA, returns are sheltered from tax, which improves the investing side of the comparison.)

The middle zone: where psychology and cash flow decide

Between the two extremes sits the debt most Canadians actually carry: a car loan at 7–8%, an unsecured line of credit at prime-plus — around 6.5% with prime at 4.45% — the provincial portion of a student loan at prime or prime-plus. (The federal portion, by the way, has carried 0% interest since April 2023 — paying that down early instead of investing is mathematically pure loss, since there's no interest to save. Priya told Jasmine exactly that about her student loan, and Jasmine pretended she'd known it all along.)

In the middle zone, the guaranteed return of payoff roughly equals the illustrative return of investing. The math calls it a coin flip — so stop asking the math and ask the human questions instead:

  • Which choice lets you sleep? Some people lie awake owing money; others lie awake watching markets wobble. Both are real costs. Price them honestly.
  • What does your cash flow need? Killing a $400/month car payment frees $400 of monthly breathing room. Investing doesn't. If your budget is tight, cash flow beats optimization.
  • How stable is your income? Debt is a fixed obligation; investments are not. A shaky job argues for fewer fixed obligations.

Nobody at a barbecue will tell you this, because "it depends on your psychology" doesn't fit on a book cover. But in the middle zone, it's the truth.

Two exceptions that beat the math

Before either path, two things outrank the whole framework:

1. If you have no emergency fund, build one first. If Sam had $0 saved and his car died, the repair would go right back on the 20% card — and the entire plan collapses. Before killing debt or investing, park a starter buffer — $1,000 to $2,000, or one month of essential expenses — in a plain savings account. It's not optimizing; it's preventing the next emergency from becoming 20% debt.

2. If your employer matches retirement contributions, take the free money first. Say Sam's warehouse offers 50 cents on the dollar up to 6% of salary in the group RRSP. That's an instant 50% return — it beats the credit card math, the mortgage math, all of it. Contribute enough to capture the full match before anything else, then run the framework on the remaining dollars. Free money doesn't get debated.

Kill it IF / invest IF

The blanket rule, replaced with something honest:

Kill the debt IF: - The rate is high — roughly 8% and up. Credit cards, payday loans, high-rate lines of credit. No contest. - You have no emergency buffer — build that first, then kill the debt. - The monthly payments are strangling your cash flow. - You lie awake thinking about owing money. Psychology is a real return.

Invest the difference IF: - The debt is low-rate — roughly 5% or less, like a mortgage at today's rates — and the guaranteed saving is small. - You already have an emergency fund, and you've captured the full employer match. - You can automate the investing so it actually happens. - Your time horizon is long. Sam is 45 — roughly twenty years to 65 — which is exactly why his catch-up instinct isn't crazy. Time is what makes the investing side of the bet reasonable.

The verdict: a framework, not a rule

"Pay off all debt before investing" is half right, and the half that's right is very right: 20% debt is an emergency, full stop. But the half that's wrong is expensive. Telling someone with a 4.3% mortgage and twenty years of compounding ahead of them to wait until the mortgage is gone before investing a dollar isn't discipline — it's the blanket rule charging tuition.

Elena's hindsight, for what it's worth: "Your father and I treated every debt like an emergency and invested the leftovers. The leftovers were smaller than we thought."

This is the framework, not personal financial advice — the rate decides, the guarantees matter, and every assumption stays labelled so you can change it.
