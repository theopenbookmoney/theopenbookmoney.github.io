Lump Sum or Drip-Feed? The Investing Question With a Data Answer

October 2026

Jasmine is inspired by a real person — names and details changed so the story can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real 2026 data.

The $6,000 question

Jasmine did her taxes in March and got a surprise: a $6,000 refund. Her biggest windfall ever.

She stared at the deposit in her savings account for two weeks, paralyzed. She'd just opened her first TFSA because Priya nagged her into it, and she knew the money should go there. But how?

"Do I dump all six grand in at once," she asked Priya, "or drip it in, like five hundred a month?"

Priya's coworker friend had an opinion: "Markets could crash tomorrow! Drip it in, it's safer." A guy at the café had the opposite one: "Time in the market beats timing the market! All at once!"

Two confident people. Opposite advice. Jasmine asked Priya, and Priya — spreadsheet keeper, jargon killer — had something better than opinions. She had data.

The question, stated plainly

You have a lump of money sitting in cash — a tax refund, a bonus, a small inheritance. Two choices:

  1. All at once (lump sum): invest the whole pile today.
  2. Drip-feed (dollar-cost averaging): split it into 12 pieces and invest one piece a month.

Everyone has a theory about which is better. The good news: someone actually measured it.

The data

In 2023, Vanguard — one of the world's biggest investment firms — published a study that tested this exact question. They looked at real market data from 1976 to 2022, across three countries: the US, the UK, and Australia. Nearly half a century of real crashes, booms, and boring years.

Their finding: putting the lump sum in immediately beat spreading it out over 12 months about 68% of the time — and won by about 2.3 percentage points on average.

Read that again. Roughly two times out of three, "all at once" earned more. The extra was about two cents on the dollar — not life-changing, but real.

Why "all at once" usually wins: one sentence

Markets go up more often than they go down. So money that gets in earlier spends more time growing.

That's the whole reason. Drip-feeding means some of your money sits in cash for months, earning almost nothing, while the market — more often than not — climbs without it. It's like showing up late to a long escalator and waiting at the bottom just in case it breaks.

What "68% of the time" really means

68% is not 100%. Here's what it means in plain words:

Out of 100 parallel-universe Jasmines, each getting a $6,000 refund in a different year, about 68 would have ended up ahead investing it all at once. About 32 would have done better dripping it in — usually because the market dropped right after they invested, and the drip-feeders bought in cheaper along the way.

And "2.3 percentage points on average" is an average. In some years the lump sum crushed it; in crash years the drip-feed won by a mile. Averages hide the scary years.

The honest catch: drip-feeding is regret insurance

Here's the part the numbers don't capture: how you would feel.

Imagine Jasmine invests all $6,000 on a Monday. On Tuesday, the market falls 20%. For two years, her statement is a sea of red. Every time she looks at it, she thinks: "If I'd dripped it in, half of this would have been invested after the crash, at bargain prices."

That pain is real. And avoiding it is exactly what drip-feeding is for.

Think of it this way: drip-feeding is regret insurance. You pay a small premium — the extra return you probably give up, about 2 cents on the dollar — in exchange for sleeping better if the market drops tomorrow. Insurance isn't about the highest return. It's about the return you can live with.

All at once fits IF you can stomach a crash right after investing without panicking and selling. Dripping it in fits IF you'd lie awake worrying, or — worse — the worry would make you sell. The best strategy is the one you'll actually stick with. A lump sum you panic-sell in a crash beats nothing; a drip-feed you stay calm with beats a lump sum you bail on.

The distinction most people get wrong

Now the important part, in plain words.

Drip-feeding only counts if you HAVE the lump sum sitting in cash and CHOOSE to stretch it out. That's what the study measured: cash in hand, on purpose, held back month by month.

Money from each paycheque is something completely different. You don't have that money yet — you get it every two weeks. Investing it as it arrives isn't drip-feeding; it's investing as you earn, and it's always good. It's what every retirement plan on earth is built on.

So if someone tells you "I dollar-cost average into my RRSP every payday," they're using the wrong word. They don't have a lump sum to begin with. They're doing the right thing — investing the money as soon as they get it — and they should keep doing it.

This article is only about one situation: the money is already sitting there, in a lump, and you're deciding how fast to put it to work.

The verdict

The data has a clear answer: invest it all at once, about two-thirds of the time, for a bit more money.

But data doesn't feel regret. If investing it all at once means a crash would make you panic, sell, and never invest again — drip it in. The 2 cents on the dollar you give up is cheap compared to a lifetime of never investing at all.

Jasmine's choice? She asked Priya what she should do. Priya laughed and said the one thing no data study can tell you: "Which version of you sleeps at night — the one who jumped in, or the one who waded in?"

Jasmine waded in. Six months, $1,000 a month. Not because it was optimal — she knows it probably wasn't — but because it was the plan she'd actually keep. And the plan you keep beats the perfect plan you abandon.
