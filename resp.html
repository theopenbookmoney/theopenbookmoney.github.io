Free Money for Your Kid's Education

October 2026

Rob and Priya are inspired by real people — names and details changed so their stories can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real 2026 data or a clearly labelled assumption.

The most expensive sentence at school pickup

Maya is 8. Liam is 5. Last month, at school pickup, another parent said the sentence that ruined Rob's week: "University costs about thirty grand a year now. And that's if they live at home."

Rob did the math on the drive home. Two kids. Four years each. He stopped doing the math.

That night Priya said, "There's a thing for this. It's called an RESP. And the government puts in free money."

Rob was skeptical. "Nothing from the government is free."

"This is," Priya said. "Let me open the book on it."

What an RESP is

An RESP — Registered Education Savings Plan — is a savings account for a child's education. University, college, trade school, CEGEP. Any real post-secondary program.

You put money in. It grows without tax touching it. When your kid starts school, the money comes out to pay for it.

That's it. It's a box with your kid's name on it.

One honest note first: unlike an RRSP, you do NOT get a tax deduction when you put money in. You contribute dollars you've already paid tax on. The reward comes later — and the biggest reward isn't the tax shelter. It's the free money.

The free money: 20 cents on every dollar

This is the part that matters. It's called the Canada Education Savings Grant. Here's how it works:

For every dollar you put into the RESP, the government adds 20 cents. Up to $500 a year. To get the full $500, you put in $2,500 that year.

Put in $2,500 → the government adds $500. Put in $1,000 → the government adds $200. It's always 20%.

Over a child's lifetime, the government will add up to $7,200 per kid. Free.

Read that again. $7,200 per child, for saving money you were going to save anyway.

(If your family income is lower, the deal is even better: an extra 10 or 20 cents on the first $500 each year. The income lines shift a bit every year, so check the current ones on canada.ca.)

Rob and Priya's numbers

Labelled assumptions: Maya is 8. Rob and Priya put $2,500 a year into her RESP, every year until she's 17. That's 10 years.
Their contributions: $2,500 × 10 years $25,000
Government grant: $500 × 10 years $5,000
Total before any growth $30,000

$30,000. From $25,000 of their own money. The government handed them $5,000 for showing up.

And that's before the money grows. Inside the RESP, every dollar of growth — interest, dividends, gains — compounds with no tax bill each year. We're not assuming any growth rate here, because nobody can promise one. But even at zero growth, the grant alone is a 20% return on day one.

The lifetime math: to collect the full $7,200 grant takes $36,000 of contributions ($7,200 ÷ 20%). Spread over 15 years, that's $2,400 a year. Start at birth and it's about $200 a month.

Start late? The grant room carries forward one year at a time. Miss a year, and you can catch up the next year by contributing up to $5,000 — which earns a $1,000 grant that year. You can't catch up more than one year at once, so don't wait forever.

The grant stops at the end of the calendar year your child turns 17. There's also a $50,000 lifetime limit on what you can contribute per child — but the grant runs out long before that matters for most families.

The fine print: where the tax actually lands

When your kid starts school, the money comes out in two buckets:

Bucket 1: your contributions. This money was already taxed when you earned it. It comes back out tax-free. No surprises.

Bucket 2: the grant plus all the growth. This comes out as "educational assistance payments." It's taxable — but in your kid's hands, not yours. And students barely earn anything, so the tax is usually close to zero. That's the whole trick: the growth is taxed at the lowest possible rate, years later.

What if the kid doesn't go to school?

This is the fear that stops parents from opening one. So here's the honest answer:

  • Your contributions come back to you. Every dollar. No tax, no penalty. It was your money.
  • The grant goes back to the government. Fair — it was never yours.
  • The growth gets taxed in your hands, plus an extra 20% penalty if you just take the cash. But there's an escape hatch: if you have RRSP room, you can move up to $50,000 of that growth into your RRSP and skip the penalty entirely.

And the account can stay open for years — kids change their minds, take gap years, go back at 25. An RESP can stay open up to 36 years. No rush.

Worst case, you're no worse off than if you'd saved in a regular account. Best case, the government paid for a year of tuition.

Who should open one, and when

Anyone can open an RESP for a kid — parent, grandparent, doesn't matter. The child needs a Social Insurance Number, which is the only real paperwork.

When? As soon as the kid has a SIN. Every year you wait is a year of grant room you have to catch up later — and you can only catch up one year at a time.

You don't need $2,500 a year to make it worth it. Put in $600 a year ($50 a month) and the government still adds $120. It's still 20% free. Start where you are.

One piece of scenario honesty: if you're drowning in high-interest debt with zero savings, build your starter emergency buffer first — then open the RESP. The grant will still be there. Money you need next month doesn't belong in an education account.

The bottom line

The government will give your child up to $7,200 for their education. Not a loan. Not a trick. A grant — 20 cents on every dollar you save, up to $500 a year.

Rob's summary, taped next to Priya's emergency-fund note on the fridge: "We put in $2,500. They add $500. The kid pays almost no tax on it later. If she doesn't go to school, we get our money back. There is no version of this where we lose."

Open the account. Get the SIN. Start with whatever you can. The free money is waiting.
