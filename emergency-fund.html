The Emergency Fund: How Much Is Enough?

October 2026

Rob is inspired by a real person — names and details changed so the story can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real 2026 data or a clearly labelled assumption you can change in the calculator below.

The month the overtime dried up

Rob works nights at a distribution warehouse in Saskatoon. For years, overtime was just part of life. Extra shifts meant extra pay. Extra pay meant the mortgage got paid, and there was still money for hockey gear and birthday parties.

Then last fall, the overtime dried up. New manager. New rules. Rob's paycheque shrank by almost a fifth — and nothing else shrank with it. The mortgage still wanted its $1,800. The minivan still needed insurance. Maya still needed new winter boots.

That was the month Rob asked Priya the question he had avoided for a decade: "If my hours got cut for real — not just the overtime, but the job itself — how long could we keep the lights on?"

Priya opened the spreadsheet. She did the math. Then she showed him the answer, and it scared them both.

This article is that math. Every household needs it. Most don't have it.

What an emergency fund is (and isn't)

An emergency fund is money set aside for bad surprises. That is all it is. It is not vacation money. It is not a down payment. It is the money that stands between your family and debt when life breaks something.

Here is the test. Ask one question: "Did I choose this?"

  • Furnace dies in February. You did not choose that. Emergency.
  • Car needs a $900 repair so you can get to work. You did not choose that. Emergency.
  • Hours get cut at work. You did not choose that. Emergency.
  • Big sale at the mall. You chose that. Not an emergency.
  • Vacation deal you can't pass up. You chose that. Not an emergency.
  • Your friend's wedding in Banff. Nice. Still not an emergency.

If you have to talk yourself into it, it is not an emergency. Real emergencies never need a sales pitch.

The 3-to-6-month rule

Here is the rule you have probably heard: keep 3 to 6 months of essential expenses saved.

Essential expenses are the bills that don't stop: housing, food, utilities, transport, minimum debt payments. Not restaurants. Not shopping. Just what it costs to keep your life running in a bad month.

But 3 to 6 is a wide range. Which end is yours? It depends on how shaky your income is.

Aim for 6 months IF:

  • One income covers the household. If that income stops, everything stops.
  • Your work is unstable — seasonal, contract, gig work, or overtime-heavy like Rob's.
  • You own a home. The furnace, the roof, the water heater — those are your problems now, and they are expensive ones.
  • You're self-employed. No shifts, no pay.

3 months is enough IF:

  • Two stable incomes cover the bills, and one alone could carry the essentials for a while.
  • Your jobs are steady — the kind that doesn't disappear in a slow quarter.
  • You rent. Big surprise repairs are the landlord's problem, not yours.
  • Family nearby could genuinely help in a crisis. Be honest with yourself about this one.

Rob's house: one main income, overtime-dependent, homeowner, two kids. Priya circled the answer before he finished reading the list. Six months.

Where to keep it

This part is simple. Keep it in a plain savings account — one you can reach within a day.

Not in stocks. Not in crypto. Not locked in something you can't touch until next year. The job of this money is not to grow. The job of this money is to be there. The day the furnace dies, you don't want your emergency fund to be having a bad day in the market too.

Separate account. Boring account. That is the whole strategy.

One more thing: don't keep it in your everyday chequing account. Money you see every day gets spent. Give it its own account. Priya named theirs "Do Not Touch." Rob has touched it zero times in two years. The name works.

What if you have debt too?

This is the question everyone asks next. "I owe money — shouldn't every spare dollar go to the debt?"

Short answer: build a small buffer first, then attack the debt.

Here is why. If you have zero savings and the car breaks down, the repair goes on a credit card. Now you have the same emergency plus 20% interest. A starter buffer of $1,000 to $2,000 breaks that cycle. It is not your full emergency fund. It is a shield while you fight the debt.

Then do both: put most of your extra money toward the expensive debt — credit cards, payday loans, anything with a high rate — and keep adding a little to the buffer each month. Once the expensive debt is gone, finish building the full 3-to-6-month fund.

The logic matches our debt-vs-invest guide: expensive debt is an emergency wearing a disguise, so it goes first. But never with zero in reserve.

The worked example: Rob's numbers

Priya built it the way she builds everything: one column, no hiding.

Rob's essential monthly expenses (labelled assumptions — swap in your own):

Expense Per month
Mortgage $1,800
Property tax + home insurance $400
Groceries $900
Utilities + phones $450
Car (payment + insurance + gas) $700
Kids' basics $300
Total essentials $4,550

The targets:

  • 3 months: $13,650
  • 6 months (Rob's number): $27,300

Where Rob actually was: $6,000 in the "Do Not Touch" account.

$6,000 ÷ $4,550 = 1.3 months covered.

Read that again. If Rob's income stopped completely, the family had about five weeks before the money ran out. Not five months. Five weeks.

The shortfall to even the 3-month minimum: $13,650 − $6,000 = $7,650.

At $500 a month saved, that gap closes in about 15 months. At $750 a month, about 10 months. Priya put both numbers on the fridge. Rob picked $750. The overtime came back that spring, and every extra shift went straight to "Do Not Touch" until it hit $13,650.

He is still building toward six. But he sleeps better at 3 than he did at 1.3.

What Elena says

Elena, 67, Rob's mother, listened to all of this and nodded slowly.

"Your father and I never had one," she said. "Every emergency went on a credit card. We paid interest on our bad luck for thirty years. Don't do what we did."
