The 45-Minute Document That Protects Everything

October 2026

Elena and Frank are fictional — their story is invented to make the ideas stick. Every number in this article is real 2026 data.

The call that started it

Last spring, a friend of Elena's died. He was 71. No will.

His kids spent eight months in court. They fought over the house. The court appointed someone to sort it out — not someone the family chose. The family barely speaks now.

Elena came home from the funeral and said to Frank: "We're doing ours. This week."

Frank is 70. He retired from the railway years ago. Elena is 67. They have a bungalow, some savings, two kids, two grandkids. They are not rich. They are exactly the people who need a will — because a will is not about being rich. It's about being clear.

They did it in one evening. Forty-five minutes, plus tea. This article is what they learned, and what they're telling Rob and Jasmine now.

What a will actually is

A will is a short legal document that says what happens to your stuff after you die. That's it.

It's not about wealth. It's not about lawyers in wood-panelled rooms. It's a piece of paper that answers three questions. Answer them, and your family is protected. Skip them, and someone else answers for you — and you won't like how.

Decision 1: who gets your stuff

This is the one everyone knows. Your will says who gets what: the house, the savings, the car, the good dishes.

Without it, you don't get a vote. Every province has its own default formula for dividing things up — it's called intestacy, and the formulas differ a lot from province to province. In some provinces, a common-law partner can end up with nothing at all. The formula doesn't know you wanted your daughter to have the cottage and your son to have the tools. It just divides.

A will lets you say: this goes here, that goes there. Done.

Decision 2: who raises your kids

If you have young kids, this is the most important line in the whole document.

Your will names a guardian — the person who raises your children if both parents are gone. Without a will, the court picks. The court will try its best. But the court never met your sister, doesn't know your brother is a mess, and has never heard your opinion on any of it.

Rob and Priya have Maya (8) and Liam (5). One sentence in their will names who takes the kids. That sentence is worth more than every other line combined.

Decision 3: who carries it out

Someone has to do the work after you're gone: pay the bills, close the accounts, hand out what's left. That person is called the executor.

Your will names them. Without a will, someone has to apply to the court just to be allowed to do the job — and the court might not pick the person you would have picked. It takes longer. It costs more. Everyone waits.

Pick someone organized and honest. Tell them you picked them. Tell them where the document is.

What happens if you skip all of this

Here's the short version: the province decides.

Each province has its own default rules, and they differ — what's true in Ontario isn't true in Saskatchewan or Quebec. But everywhere, the pattern is the same: a fixed formula splits your things, a judge appoints the person in charge, and nobody asks what you wanted.

And skipping it is normal, which is the scary part. Roughly half of Canadians don't have a will at all. About one in eight have one that's out of date — written before the kids, before the divorce, before the house. An out-of-date will is almost as bad as none.

Don't be half. Forty-five minutes fixes it.

The two companions (one paragraph each)

A will handles what happens after you die. Two more documents handle the rest of life.

Power of attorney. This one is for while you're alive but can't decide for yourself — a stroke, dementia, a bad accident. It names someone to handle your money and property, and someone to make health and care decisions. The key word to ask for is "continuing" or "enduring": that version keeps working even if you lose the ability to decide. Without one, your family has to go to court for permission to pay your bills — while you're in a hospital bed. Don't do that to them.

Beneficiary designations. Some of your money skips the will entirely. If you named a beneficiary on your RRSP, your TFSA, or your life insurance, that money goes straight to that person when you die — no will needed, no waiting. But only if the name on file is current. An ex-spouse from 2011 still listed as your beneficiary will get the money, and your will can't stop it. Check the names. It takes five minutes.

The 45-minute push

Elena's honest summary: "We put it off for twenty years because we thought it was complicated. It wasn't. Three decisions, two companions, one evening."

You don't need to be perfect. A simple will that answers the three questions beats a perfect plan that never gets written. If your life is complicated — a business, a blended family, property in two provinces — talk to a lawyer. For everyone else, the basics take less time than a TV episode.

The bottom line

You spent decades building what you have: the house, the savings, the family. A will is the 45-minute document that makes sure it all goes where you meant it to go — instead of where a provincial formula sends it.

Elena taped a note to the fridge, next to Priya's emergency-fund note and Rob's budget note: "We did ours. Now do yours. Forty-five minutes. No excuses."
