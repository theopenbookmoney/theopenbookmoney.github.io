Sick, Broke, and Laid Off: How EI Actually Works

October 2026

Sam is inspired by a real person — names and details changed so the story can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real 2026 data.

Sam is 45, divorced, and works the night shift at a distribution warehouse in Saskatoon with Rob. Last month the company lost a big contract and cut Sam's shift entirely. He walked out of the building with a box of his things and a question he'd never had to ask in twenty-five years of working: "What do I actually get from EI?"

Ask five people and you get five answers. His buddy at the bar said EI covers your full paycheque. His neighbour said the money starts the day you're fired. Sam's ex's cousin said you can quit and collect. All three are wrong — and believing them is how people end up with no money in month two.

Here's what EI actually is, in plain words.

What EI is (and isn't)

Employment Insurance is a program you and every employer you've ever had have been paying into with every paycheque. When you lose a job through no fault of your own, it pays you a weekly benefit while you look for work. That's it. It's a bridge, not a salary.

Three things it is not:

  • It is not your full salary. It pays 55% of your average insurable earnings — and even that is capped.
  • It is not automatic. Nobody sends you a cheque because you got laid off. You have to apply, and there's a waiting period before payments start.
  • It is not for quitting. If you quit or get fired for misconduct, you generally don't qualify at all.

Who qualifies: the hours test

The big gate is hours. You need a minimum number of insurable hours worked in the 52 weeks before you apply. Insurable hours means hours at jobs where you and your employer paid EI premiums — basically, normal payroll jobs, not cash under the table.

The exact number depends on where you live, because it's tied to the unemployment rate in your region:

  • In regions with the lowest unemployment, you need 700 hours.
  • In regions with the highest unemployment, you need 420 hours.
  • Most regions land somewhere between 420 and 700.

If you worked full-time for about half the year — say 20 hours a week for 35 weeks — you're around 700 hours. The higher the local unemployment rate, the fewer hours you need, because the government knows jobs are harder to find there.

And the job loss has to be through no fault of your own — a layoff, a shortage of work, the company closing. Quitting because you felt like it? Doesn't count. Fired for cause? Doesn't count.

What it pays: 55%, with a ceiling

EI pays 55% of your average insurable weekly earnings. Not your full pay. Just over half.

And there's a ceiling. Benefits are calculated on your earnings up to a maximum — the maximum insurable earnings for 2026 is $68,900 a year. Work that through (divide by 52), and 55% of that ceiling comes out to a maximum benefit of $729 per week.

So:

  • If you earned $1,000 a week, 55% is $550. Under the cap. You get $550.
  • If you earned $1,500 a week, 55% would be $825 — but the cap cuts you off. You get $729, the maximum.
  • If you earned $3,000 a week, you still get $729.

The higher you were paid, the smaller the fraction of your paycheque EI replaces. That's the part nobody tells you at the bar.

How long it lasts: 14 to 45 weeks

The number of weeks you can collect also depends on your hours and your region — from 14 weeks up to 45 weeks. More hours worked and higher local unemployment both push you toward the longer end.

Two catches people trip over:

  1. You must apply — and keep filing. You apply to Service Canada after you lose your job (do it as soon as possible; waiting can cost you weeks). Then you have to file reports every two weeks showing you're able, available, and looking for work. Stop filing, payments stop.
  2. There's a one-week waiting period. Your first week of unemployment is unpaid by EI — it's like a deductible. Payments start after that. This is the number-one reason people think "the money never came": they were counting days before the waiting period was over.

Sam's numbers: the worked example

Sam earned $1,100 a week at the warehouse before the layoff. Let's run the formula:

  • 55% of $1,100 = $605 a week
  • Is $605 under the $729 cap? Yes.
  • So Sam gets $605 a week, before tax.

Before tax — because yes, EI benefits are taxable income. That's myth number four: people hear "$605 a week" and plan around it, then get surprised. Your real take-home is what lands after tax.

How long does Sam get it? He worked full-time all year in Saskatoon, so he's well past the hours threshold. Depending on the regional unemployment rate at the time, he'd be looking at somewhere in the upper part of that 14-to-45-week range — roughly half a year to ten months of runway while he finds his next job.

Half a paycheque, not a full one. A bridge, not a salary. That's the honest version — and it's still better than the nothing Sam was expecting when he walked out with that box.

What people say What's true
