Your Mortgage Renewal Is a $10,000 Conversation

October 2026

Rob and Priya are inspired by real people — names and details changed so their stories can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real 2026 data.

Rob almost signed it. That's the whole story, really.

The letter came from his bank in June: his five-year mortgage term was ending in October, and here was his renewal offer — 5.14%, same bank, same mortgage, just sign on the line. One page. Four years of night shifts at the warehouse had taught Rob to finish paperwork fast and move on. He had the pen in his hand.

Priya took the letter out of his hand.

"Did you ask anyone else what they'd charge?" she said.

Rob blinked. "It's my bank. That's the rate."

Priya has kept the household spreadsheet for twelve years, and she has a rule: a number nobody negotiates is a number somebody invented. She called the bank's retention desk. She called a mortgage broker a coworker recommended. Within a week she had a second offer: 4.34%. Same $400,000 balance, same five-year term, same everything — except the number.

Rob did the math with her, and the math is why this article exists. That one letter, unchallenged, would have cost them roughly $10,000.

Two things you need to know first

A mortgage term is the length of your current deal — usually five years — after which you have to re-sign at whatever rates exist then.

Amortization is the full lifespan of the mortgage — the total number of years it takes to pay it off completely, usually 25 or 30.

At the end of every term, you renew. And at every renewal, you get a letter. Here's what the bank doesn't print on it: that letter is an opening offer, not a final price.

The letter is the worst rate they'll offer you

Think about it from the bank's side. They know three things about you: your mortgage is with them, switching takes effort, and most people never shop around. So the renewal letter quotes a rate with all of that baked in — a rate for people who won't compare it to anything.

They are not lying to you. They are betting on your inertia. It's a good bet, too: most borrowers sign the letter and never ask a single question. That's exactly why the renewal letter is the worst rate you'll be offered — the rate for the customer who doesn't shop is never the rate for the customer who does.

Priya's rule applies to more than her spreadsheet: the only way to find out the real price is to ask someone else what they'd charge.

The worked example: Rob and Priya's numbers

The setup: $400,000 balance remaining, renewing into a fresh five-year term, 25-year amortization. (Rates below are current-market illustrations — re-verify them at publish time. Your own rates will differ, but the gap between "letter rate" and "shopped rate" is the point.)

Renewal letter Shopped rate
Rate 5.14% 4.34%
Monthly payment $2,358.40 $2,178.45
Interest paid over the 5-year term $96,308.20 $80,955.98

Read those numbers slowly.

The shopped rate cuts the monthly payment by $179.95 a month — about $180. That's $10,796.96 back in your pocket over five years — money you don't hand to the bank.

And over the full five-year term, the cheaper rate means $15,352.22 less interest paid — interest that is gone forever if you pay it.

Add it up and the difference between signing the letter and shopping around is about $15,350 over five years. Make shopping a habit at every renewal and the lifetime difference on this mortgage runs past $50,000. So "a $10,000 conversation" is, if anything, an understatement.

Rob stared at the $180-a-month difference. "That's a grocery run," he said. Priya corrected him: "That's a grocery run every month, for five years, that we would have donated to the bank."

You're not alone in this — it's renewal season

Roughly 1.15 million Canadian mortgages renew in 2026, and about 940,000 more renew in 2027. That's over two million renewal letters going out — two million opening offers, and two million chances to do what Priya did instead of what Rob almost did.

You don't have to wait for the letter

Here's the part most people miss: you don't have to shop only after the letter arrives. Most lenders let you start the renewal conversation up to 120 days before your term ends — four full months. That's your window to call the bank's retention desk (the department whose whole job is keeping you from leaving), talk to a mortgage broker (who shops multiple lenders for you), or take your business to a different lender entirely.

Four months is plenty of time to get three quotes. Rob and Priya's whole comparison took one week.

What shopping actually looks like

It's less dramatic than it sounds. Three moves, in plain order:

  1. When the letter arrives, read the rate — then put the pen down. The letter is designed to be signed quickly. Don't.
  2. Call your own bank's retention desk and ask what they can do. This is not the regular phone line — it's the team that keeps customers. The rate they offer on that call is routinely lower than the rate in the letter.
  3. Get one outside quote. A mortgage broker costs you nothing out of pocket — they're paid by the lender — and one competing number is all it takes to turn the letter from a final price back into an opening offer.

That's it. No forms marathon, no loyalty test. And note: staying with your current lender after negotiating is a perfectly good outcome — the point is that the negotiated rate is the real rate, and the letter was never it.

The one-line version

Your lender's renewal letter is priced for the customer who doesn't shop. Be the customer who shops.
