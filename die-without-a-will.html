Die Without a Will and the Government Writes One for You

October 2026

Elena and Frank are inspired by real people — names and details changed so the story can be told honestly. The legal rules in this article are from Saskatchewan's The Intestate Succession Act, 2019, and the Government of Saskatchewan's guidance. The dollar examples are hypothetical and labelled as such.

Elena is 67, Frank is 70, and they live in Saskatoon. Frank retired from the railway. Elena worked retail. They are comfortable, not rich, and they have never made a will. Every time Elena brings it up, Frank says the same thing:

"Don't worry. If something happens to me, it all goes to you. Obviously."

Elena is the one who reads every statement twice. So she asked the question Frank's confidence skipped over: obviously according to who?

According to the Government of Saskatchewan, the answer is: sometimes. Not always. And that is the whole problem with dying without a will. You don't get to say who gets what. A formula says it for you — and the formula has never met your family.

The one idea

When someone in Saskatchewan dies without a valid will, they are said to die "intestate." Their estate — everything they owned, minus debts, funeral costs, and the cost of administering the estate — is divided by a set of rules in a law called The Intestate Succession Act, 2019. That law came into force on October 1, 2019, replacing an older law from 1996.

The formula is not random. It is a reasonable guess at what most people would want. But it is still a guess. It does not know that you raised your stepdaughter since she was four. It does not know the cottage matters more than the money. It does not know about the friend you promised something to. A will is the only place those things get said.

Here is the formula, in plain words.

The formula, case by case

A spouse, and no children. Everything goes to the spouse. This is the case Frank was picturing — and it is true, as far as it goes.

A spouse, and children — where every child is also the spouse's child. Everything goes to the spouse. All of it. The children get nothing from this estate under the formula. The idea is that the surviving parent will provide for the children and leave whatever remains to them later, in their own will. But that second part only happens if the surviving spouse actually makes a will.

A spouse, and children — where at least one child is not the spouse's child. This is the blended-family case, and it is where the formula gets complicated. The spouse first takes a "preferential share": the greater of $200,000 or half of the estate's net value. Then whatever is left is split:

  • One child: half to the spouse, half to the child.
  • Two or more children: one-third to the spouse, two-thirds shared equally among the children.

If one of the children has already died but has children of their own, that dead child's share goes to their children instead.

No spouse, but children. Everything is split equally among the children, with a dead child's share going to that child's children.

No spouse and no children. The estate walks down the family tree in this order: first to the parents, then to the parents' other descendants (brothers, sisters, and their children), then to grandparents and their descendants (aunts, uncles, and cousins), then to great-grandparents and their descendants, and so on.

The worked example: one difference changes everything

This is a made-up example, with round numbers, to show how the formula works. Assume an estate worth $600,000 after debts, funeral costs, and administration costs are paid. (The law calls this the "net value." In real life the preferential share also carries interest; this example leaves interest out to keep the arithmetic clear.)

Version one: a blended family. Suppose the person who died had a spouse and two children, and the two children are not the spouse's children.

  • Preferential share: the greater of $200,000 or half of $600,000 — so $300,000 to the spouse.
  • Remainder: $300,000. The spouse gets one-third of it: $100,000. The children share two-thirds: $200,000, or $100,000 each.
  • Totals: spouse $400,000, each child $100,000.

Version two: the same $600,000, but both children are also the spouse's children.

  • Totals: spouse $600,000, each child $0.

Same money. Same number of children. One difference — whose children they are — and the result flips completely. In version one, the children get a share no matter what the surviving spouse thinks. In version two, the children get nothing unless the surviving spouse chooses to leave it to them later.

Read that again, because it is the sentence the whole article turns on: the formula treats "your children" and "your spouse's children" as different things, and most families never notice until it is too late.

Four things the formula does not know

It does not know your stepchildren. A stepchild — your spouse's child, who you did not adopt — gets nothing under the formula. An adopted child, on the other hand, counts exactly the same as a biological child. So the child you raised since she was four but never formally adopted inherits nothing, while the child you adopted inherits equally. The formula draws its lines on paper, not on love.

It does not know your common-law partner — unless you lived together long enough. In Saskatchewan, "spouse" in this law means a legally married spouse, or a partner who lived with the person as spouses continuously for at least two years and was still living with them when they died (or had stopped living with them within the last 24 months). Short of that, a long-term partner the law does not recognize as a spouse gets nothing under the formula — no matter how many years they shared a life, unless a court orders otherwise on a dependant's claim.

It does not know you separated. If a married couple had been living separate and apart for two years when one of them died, the surviving spouse gets nothing under the formula — even though they are still legally married. Still married is not the same as still a spouse, in this law's eyes.

It does not divide everything you owned. The formula only divides what is actually in the estate. Property owned jointly with someone else usually passes straight to the surviving joint owner. Bank accounts, RRSPs, TFSAs, and insurance policies with a named beneficiary go straight to that beneficiary. So the house you own jointly with your spouse may already be covered — but the bank account in your name alone, and the investments with no beneficiary named, are not. "Most of it is joint" is not the same as "all of it is handled."

Who runs the show when there is no will

A will names an executor: the person who carries it out. Without a will, there is no executor. Instead, someone — usually the spouse first, then children, then parents, then siblings — has to apply to the Court of King's Bench for Saskatchewan to be appointed administrator, through a document called Letters of Administration. That takes time, paperwork, and money, and the administrator has to track down every beneficiary before anything can be distributed.

There is one small mercy: if the estate is worth $25,000 or less and includes no real property, Letters of Administration may not be required at all. But a house alone puts most estates well past that line.

The Government of Saskatchewan puts the cost of skipping a will plainly: without one, these matters may be decided by the court instead, which can lead to costly and lengthy proceedings and family disputes over who gets what. And one more honest note from the same source: even the formula is not the last word. A dependant can claim support from the estate, and a spouse can claim a division of family property — either of which can change the result.

One more thing: older guides say $100,000, and they are out of date

If you read an older article or guide that says the spouse's preferential share is $100,000, it is not wrong — it is old. That was the figure under the 1996 law. The 2019 law raised it to $200,000 (or half the estate, whichever is greater), and the new rules apply to deaths on or after October 1, 2019. If you are reading a guide that still says $100,000, check its date before you trust anything else in it.

The verdict: the formula is fair, and it is not yours

Here is the kindest true thing to say about Saskatchewan's formula: for a simple family, it probably lands close to what you would have chosen anyway. Spouse gets everything; children share equally; parents next. Sensible.

But "close to what you would have chosen" is not the same as "what you chose." The formula cannot leave the cottage to one child and the savings to another. It cannot provide for a stepchild. It cannot leave anything to a friend, a charity, or the neighbour who checked in every week. It cannot name who raises your grandchildren. And in a blended family, it can hand a third of the remainder to a spouse your children barely know, or hand everything to a spouse and leave your children hoping.

Frank's version — "it all goes to you, obviously" — is true exactly when there are no children, or when every child is also the spouse's. In every other shape of family, "obviously" does a lot of quiet damage.
