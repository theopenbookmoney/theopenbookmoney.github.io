The HELOC Is a Credit Card Hiding in Your House

October 2026

Rob and Priya are inspired by real people — names and details changed so their stories can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real 2026 data.

Rob and Priya have lived in their Saskatoon house for eleven years. Their mortgage is doing fine. The kids are growing. Life is normal.

Then the bank called.

It wasn't a scary call. It was a friendly call. The bank told Rob that his house is now worth a lot more than he owes on it, and that equity — the gap between what the house is worth and what they still owe — could be his money. Up to $60,000 of it, available whenever he wants. Just sign here, and the bank will open a home equity line of credit — a HELOC.

Rob hung up feeling like he'd just won a small lottery. Free money, sitting inside his own walls.

He told Priya. Priya, who opens the CRA letters and keeps the household spreadsheet, said six words: "It's not free. Read the fine print."

She was right. And the fine print is why this article exists.

What a HELOC actually is

A HELOC is a line of credit secured against your home. Think of it like this: the bank puts a tap on the side of your house. You can turn the tap whenever you want and draw money out, up to your limit. As you repay, the room opens back up and you can draw again.

That last part is what makes it different from a mortgage — and what makes it dangerous. A mortgage is a one-time loan you pay down until it's gone. A HELOC is revolving: pay some back, borrow it again tomorrow. It works exactly like a credit card.

Except the collateral isn't nothing. It's your house.

The fine print Rob almost signed past:

  • The revolving portion is capped at 65% of your home's value. You can't borrow the full equity.
  • Your mortgage and your HELOC combined can't exceed 80% of your home's value. That's a federal rule — it's the ceiling no lender can cross.
  • The rate is usually variable. It moves up and down with the Bank of Canada's policy rate. When rates rise, your HELOC payment rises with them.
  • Minimum payments are usually interest-only. This is the big one. Read it again.

The trap: interest-only minimums never shrink anything

Here's what "interest-only" means in plain English.

On a credit card, the minimum payment is small, but at least a slice of it chips away at what you owe. On most HELOCs, the minimum payment covers only the interest — the full $50,000 you borrowed just sits there, untouched, waiting for you next month. And the month after. And the month after that.

You can pay faithfully for five years, ten years, twenty years — and owe exactly the same amount as the day you started. The debt doesn't shrink. It just rents you money, monthly, forever.

And because the rate is variable, "forever" gets more expensive whenever the Bank of Canada raises rates. Your minimum can go up while your balance stays frozen. You're paying more for the privilege of standing still.

The bank's friendly call never mentioned any of this. Friendly calls never do.

The worked example: Rob's $50,000

Priya ran the numbers the way she runs everything — in the spreadsheet, no feelings involved.

Say Rob draws $50,000 from the HELOC at 7% interest. (The monthly payment math here is the article's worked example, shown step by step so you can check it.)

If he pays only the minimum — the interest-only amount:

  • $50,000 × 7% ÷ 12 months = about $292 a month.
  • After one year of paying $292 every month, he owes $50,000.
  • After five years: $50,000.
  • After ten years: $50,000.
  • Twenty years of payments adds up to roughly $70,000 paid — and he still owes the original $50,000.

Read that again. $70,000 out the door, balance unchanged. That's not a loan. That's a subscription to debt.

If he pays $600 a month instead:

  • The first month's interest is still about $292. But now $308 extra goes straight at the $50,000 — and the balance drops to roughly $49,692.
  • Next month the interest is charged on $49,692 instead of $50,000 — a little less. A little more of the $600 hits the principal. Then more. Then more.
  • The $50,000 is fully gone in about 9½ years (115 months).
  • Total interest paid: roughly $18,700 — about a quarter of what the forever-minimums path costs over twenty years, with nothing left owing at the end.

Same house. Same $50,000. Same 7%. The only difference is the payment plan — and the difference is tens of thousands of dollars plus the freedom of actually being done.

When a HELOC is genuinely useful

This is not an anti-HELOC article. The editorial rule here is scenario honesty: a HELOC is a fine tool in the right scenario.

A HELOC is great IF:

  • It's one-time, short-term, and paid off fast. The classic honest use: a renovation that raises the home's value — say a kitchen update before you sell. You borrow, you do the work, you repay it from the higher sale price or within a couple of years. The tool did its job and left.
  • You have a firm repayment plan with a finish line. "We're paying $600 a month and it's gone in under ten years" is a plan. "We'll see how it goes" is not.
  • You can handle a rate increase. The rate is variable. Ask yourself: if it went to 9%, would the payments still fit? If the answer is no, the answer is no.

When to walk away

A HELOC is dangerous IF:

  • It's funding lifestyle spending. A vacation, a new truck, Christmas on the house's tab. The house didn't earn that money — you just mortgaged your walls to feel richer for a week. Interest-only minimums make it feel free. It is not free.
  • You're consolidating debt you'll just re-accumulate. This is the classic trap. People roll $30,000 of credit-card debt into a HELOC at a lower rate, feel the relief, then slowly refill the credit cards — because the spending habit never changed. Now they owe the HELOC and the cards. The lower rate didn't save them; it just made borrowing feel safe enough to do twice.
  • You're treating equity like income. "Our house went up $80,000 this year, so we can afford the renovation." That's not income. That's paper. It only becomes real when you sell — and borrowing against it today bets that tomorrow's value holds.

Priya's rule, which Rob now quotes back to anyone who'll listen: "A HELOC is a shovel. A shovel is great for digging a foundation. It's terrible for digging your way out of a hole."

The verdict: it behaves like a credit card — price it like one

Strip away the friendly bank call and the word "equity," and the machine is simple: revolving credit, usually variable rate, minimum payments that don't shrink the balance, secured by the roof over your kids' heads.

Use it like a credit card you'd pay off fast — a bridge, a one-time job, a short stay. Never use it like free money, because the one thing free money doesn't do is charge you $292 a month for twenty years and hand you back the same $50,000 you started with.
