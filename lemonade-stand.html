Maya's Lemonade Stand: Teaching Kids About Money Before the World Does

October 2026

Maya, Liam, Rob and Priya are fictional — their story is invented to make the math stick. Every number in this article is real 2026 data.

It was a hot Saturday in July, and Maya had a plan.

She'd been asking Rob for a lemonade stand since May. "Dad, I can do the lemons. Liam can hold the sign." Liam was five. He could hold the sign.

Rob said yes, but Priya had a condition. "If you're going to run a business," she told Maya, "you're going to run it properly."

By 10 a.m., the table was out on the lawn. A pitcher of lemonade, a stack of paper cups, and a hand-lettered sign that read LEMONAID — 50¢. Liam had been in charge of the lettering, and honestly, the misspelling sold more cups than the lemonade.

By noon, the pitcher was empty. Maya counted her money on the kitchen table, twice, very carefully.

"Twenty-three dollars," she whispered, like she'd found treasure. "I'm rich."

Priya smiled. "Almost," she said. "Before you're rich, we need three jars."

Idea 1: Every dollar gets a job — the three jars

Priya put three mason jars on the table and taped labels on them: SPEND, SAVE, SHARE.

"This is how money works in our house," she said. "Every dollar that comes in gets split. Not later. Right now, before anything gets spent."

The rule she gave Maya is dead simple:

  • Spend — the jar for things she wants today. A book, a toy, candy at the corner store.
  • Save — the jar for things she wants later. A bike, a bigger goal, money that just sits and grows up.
  • Share — the jar for giving away. The food bank, a friend who needs something, a cause she cares about.

Maya split her $23 three ways. She wanted to put it all in spend — "it's my money" — but Priya didn't lecture. She just pointed at the jars.

"The rule works when you don't want to follow it," Priya said. "That's the whole point."

Rob watched Liam try to stuff a toonie into the SHARE jar sideways and decided this was the best $23 he'd never spent.

The three jars are the entire financial system in miniature. Money comes in, money goes to jobs, money goes out. Adults just have more complicated jars.

Idea 2: Some chores earn money. Some are just being family.

The next week, Maya tried to negotiate.

"I did the dishes last night," she told Rob. "That's a dollar."

Rob shook his head. "Dishes are family work. You live here too."

This is the second lesson, and parents get it wrong in both directions. There are two kinds of chores, and kids should know which is which.

Family work — things you do because you're part of the house. Dishes, making your bed, cleaning your own room. Nobody pays you for these. Adults don't get paid for laundry either.

Paid work — extra jobs that go above and beyond. Washing the car, helping a neighbour rake leaves, running the lemonade stand. These earn money.

Why does the split matter? Because kids who get paid for everything learn to ask "what do I get?" before doing anything decent. And kids who get paid for nothing never learn that extra effort earns extra money.

Maya wasn't thrilled. But she washed the car the next day — and earned three dollars. She split them three ways into the jars without being asked.

Idea 3: Let them make the $5 mistake now

Two weeks later, Maya spent her entire SPEND jar on a glittery pen set at the corner store. Six dollars. She used them for one afternoon. Then the sparkle wore off, literally and figuratively.

"I should have saved for the art kit," she said that night, staring at the pens.

Rob almost said the thing parents say — I told you so. Priya gave him a look. So instead he said: "What would you do differently?"

"Wait a week," Maya said. "Then see if I still want it."

That $6 regret was the cheapest money lesson of her life.

Here's the idea in one sentence: a $5 mistake at 8 beats a $5,000 mistake at 28.

A kid who learns "wait a week" on a pen set becomes an adult who waits a month on a car they can't afford. A kid who never gets to waste money on small things becomes an adult who wastes money on big things — because the first time they ever feel that regret, the number has three extra zeros.

The rule for parents: never rescue the jars. Don't top up SPEND when it's empty. The empty jar is the teacher.

Idea 4: Starting at 8 beats starting at 28

Maya's SAVE jar had $8 in it by the end of July. Rob looked at those eight crumpled dollars and thought about his own twenties — the decade he spent nodding along whenever anyone said "RRSP season," saving nothing.

He didn't lecture Maya. He just said one thing.

"Money you save young has decades to grow. Money you save at 30 has years. The same dollar does way more work when it's young."

That's compound interest in one sentence, and an 8-year-old can understand it: time is the secret ingredient, and you have more of it than anyone.

Maya didn't open a TFSA that day. She put the $8 back in the jar. But the idea landed. Years from now, when someone explains investing to her, she'll already know the most important part — start early.

Rob started at 38. Maya starts at 8. Thirty years is a gift no interest rate can match.

The closing thought

The lemonade stand is gone. The sign is in the garage. But the three jars are still on Maya's dresser, and Liam has started asking for his own — he wants the labels drawn with dinosaurs.

You don't need a perfect system. You need three jars, a split between family work and paid work, the courage to let them blow $6 on glittery pens, and one true sentence about time.

The world is going to teach your kids about money eventually. It will teach them through ads, through friends, through credit card offers that arrive like birthday cards at 18.

Teach them first. With lemons.
