The 3.9% Rule: How Much Can You Spend Without Running Out?

October 2026

Nadia is inspired by a real person — names and details changed so the story can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real 2026 data.

Nadia is 58. She's a school division administrator in Saskatoon, married, with two grown kids — one just finished university, one still in it. She and her husband did everything they were told to do: steady jobs, a modest condo nearly paid off, RRSP contributions most years.

Now retirement is seven years away, and she's doing the anxious math out loud at every family dinner.

"I've spent my whole adult life saving," she told her brother Frank last month. "And now nobody can tell me the one number I actually need: once I retire, how much of it can I spend each year without running out?"

That question is why this article exists. It's the flip side of everything the retirement article covered — that one was about building the pile. This one is about spending the pile.

The question, stated plainly

When you retire, your savings have to do two jobs at once: pay you every year, and last as long as you do. Spend too much, and the money dies before you do. Spend too little, and you die with a fortune you were afraid to enjoy.

So there's a real question underneath Nadia's worry, and it has a real answer: what percentage of your savings can you take out in year one of retirement, then keep spending (adjusted for inflation) for 30 years, with a high chance of not running out?

In 2026, the best-researched answer is 3.9%.

Where the 3.9% comes from

You may have heard of the old "4% rule." It came from research in the 1990s: withdraw 4% in your first year of retirement, then raise it with inflation each year, and your money should last 30 years.

Morningstar — a big independent investment research firm — updates this math every year in its State of Retirement Income research. For 2026, their answer is 3.9%, with a 90% chance of success over a 30-year retirement. That's up slightly from 3.7% in 2025, because expected market returns and bond yields look a little better this year.

Read that carefully: 90% chance. Not a guarantee. It means that across every nasty market scenario history can throw at the model — crashes, long flat stretches, bad luck retiring right before a downturn — 9 times out of 10, the money lasts 30 years.

And the man who invented the 4% rule, Bill Bengen, went back and revised his own work: using better data and small-cap stocks, he found the true worst-case safe rate was actually 4.7%. So Morningstar's 3.9% is the careful answer — it's built to survive even the worst years on record.

The key insight: it's a starting rate, not a flat rate

Here's the part most people miss. The 3.9% is what you take out in year one. After that, you don't take 3.9% of what's left — you take last year's amount, adjusted for inflation.

Nadia's example, in plain numbers: if she retires with $800,000 saved, she takes out $31,200 in her first year — that's $800,000 × 3.9%, about $2,600 a month. The next year, if inflation was 2%, she takes out about $31,824. And so on, for 30 years.

The rate looks small. That's the point. It's small on purpose — because it has to survive 30 years of bad markets, inflation, and you living longer than you expect.

Why you might safely spend more: the guardrails trick

The 3.9% assumes you blindly raise your spending with inflation every year, even in a market crash. But real people don't do that. When markets crash, most retirees cut back on trips and big purchases automatically.

Morningstar tested that. If you follow a simple flexible rule — take your inflation raise in good years, skip it after bad ones (researchers call these "guardrails" strategies) — the same portfolio can support a starting rate of around 5.7% instead of 3.9%.

That's a big difference on Nadia's $800,000: $45,600 a year instead of $31,200. The price is flexibility — you have to actually cut back when markets are ugly, not just promise yourself you will.

The honest scenario advice: if you can live with spending that goes up and down, you can start higher. If you need every dollar steady and predictable, stay near 3.9%.

The Canadian wrinkle: RRIF rules can force your hand

Here's something the American research doesn't cover, and it matters for Nadia. In Canada, by December 31 of the year you turn 71, your RRSP must become a RRIF — and a RRIF comes with mandatory minimum withdrawals set by law. At 71, the minimum is 5.28% of the account value that year, and it rises as you age.

That means in your 70s, the law can force you to take out more than the 3.9% guideline each year. You don't have to spend it — you can reinvest the extra in a TFSA or taxable account — but you can't leave it in the RRIF. Plan for this now, while Nadia still has seven working years to shape which accounts hold her savings.

The worked example: Nadia's number

Nadia and her husband are on track for roughly $800,000 in retirement savings (RRSPs, a workplace plan, some TFSA room — the ordinary Canadian mix). Here's what the 3.9% rule tells her:

  • Year-one spending money from savings: $31,200 — about $2,600 a month.
  • That's the amount with a 90% chance of lasting 30 years, through every market storm on record.
  • This is on top of CPP and OAS, which are separate — the 3.9% only covers what her own savings have to deliver.
  • If she and her husband can be flexible in bad market years, a guardrails strategy could stretch that toward $45,600 a year instead.

$2,600 a month from savings. Not a fortune — but it's a real number she can plan around, instead of the anxious fog she's been living in. "That's the first time anyone has given me an actual number," she told Frank.

The verdict: a starting point, not a verdict

The 3.9% rule isn't a law of nature. It's a researched starting point — the careful answer for someone who wants their money to survive 30 years of whatever the market throws at them. Flexibility buys you more. Canadian RRIF rules may force more out of you anyway.

But every retiree deserves what Nadia finally got: an actual number, from real data, that she can hold in her hand and plan around.
