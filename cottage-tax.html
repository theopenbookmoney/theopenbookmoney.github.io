Your Cottage Is a Tax Bill

October 2026

Elena and Frank are fictional — their story is invented to make the math stick. Every number in this article is real 2026 data.

Elena and Frank are 67 and 70. Frank retired from the railway. Elena worked retail. They live in the house in Saskatoon they've owned for decades, and they love their summers at the cottage — the little place two hours north, bought back in 2000, where the grandkids learned to swim off the dock.

Last winter, over coffee, a friend asked the question that started this article: "When you sell the cottage someday, what do you think it'll cost you?"

Frank said, "Nothing. It's ours." Elena, the worrier, wasn't so sure. She was right to worry.

The family heirloom that isn't

To the family, the cottage is memories. The first loon call of the morning. Frank's terrible campfire songs. A place the kids will fight over lovingly someday.

To the tax system, the cottage is something else entirely: an asset that went up in value. And unlike your house, its gains are not protected — not unless you give it the shield, and there's only one shield to go around.

The one shield rule

Here's the whole idea in one paragraph.

Canada lets you shelter the gain on your home from tax. That's the principal residence exemption — the rule that says your house can grow in value and the taxman gets none of it. The catch: you can only claim that shelter on one property per year. One shield, one property, per year.

Elena and Frank's house has been their principal residence every year they've owned it. The shield went to the house, every year, by default. That means the cottage's gains have been building up, year after year, unprotected.

Most cottage owners never realize this until someone asks, like Frank's friend did: "Which property got the shield all those years?" The answer is usually the house. Which means the cottage is carrying decades of taxable gain.

The worked example: Elena and Frank's cottage

Let's run their real numbers.

  • Bought the cottage in 2000 for $150,000
  • Worth today: $600,000
  • Gain: $600,000 − $150,000 = $450,000

The exemption covered the house for most of those years — which is what most families do — so a large slice of that $450,000 gain is taxable.

At the 50% inclusion rate (the current 2026 rate), half the gain is added to income in the year of sale: $225,000 lands on their tax return in that single year.

Read that again. Not spread across the 26 years they owned it. All of it, in one year. That's how the rule works — the tax arrives when you sell.

And a year where your income suddenly jumps by $225,000 is a year where your tax rate jumps too. Big gains can push you into higher tax brackets, and for retirees they can claw back benefits like OAS. The cottage you owned for decades can, in one year, cost you a new roof, a new car, or a year of retirement income — in tax.

The sentence that matters most

Every year you don't decide which property gets the exemption is a year the decision gets made for you by default.

You don't actually have to decide today. But understand this: each year the shield quietly goes to the house, because that's where you live. The cottage's taxable gain grows by another year's worth. The default is doing the deciding.

The earlier you look at this with actual numbers — ideally with an accountant, before anyone sells anything — the more room you have to plan. The math only gets heavier with time.

Two things to do right now

Keep your records. Every receipt that proves what the cottage cost you shrinks the gain. The purchase price. The new roof in 2012. The deck rebuild. The septic upgrade. These are called improvements, and they raise your cost base — which lowers the taxable gain. Elena reads every statement twice; this is where that habit pays. Without receipts, the cost base is just the original $150,000, and the gain stays as big as it can be.

Gifting the cottage to the kids is not a loophole. A lot of families plan to hand the cottage to the kids someday and skip the tax. It doesn't work. The tax system treats a gift as if you sold it for market value. Handing the cottage to the kids triggers the same tax as selling it to a stranger. Know that before the family makes plans around the kitchen table.

Great IF… / skip IF…

This article is for the cottage owner, so here's the honest split:

The cottage is a reasonable plan IF:

  • You've done the math and can pay the bill. The tax is real, but so are 26 years of summers. If the numbers fit, the cottage did its job. Knowing the bill in advance is what turns a surprise into a plan.
  • You have records. The better your receipts, the smaller the gain. This is the cheapest tax planning there is — a folder.
  • You've talked to an accountant before you sell or gift. The exemption years, the improvements, the timing — all of it can be optimized, but only before the transaction happens. After is just arithmetic.

This warning is not for you IF:

  • You don't own a second property. If the cottage is the only property you own, the exemption can cover it. This whole article is about having two properties and one shield.
  • You sell in a year of low income. Retirees with modest income pay lower rates on the gain. Still taxable — just at a friendlier rate.
  • You've never had a cottage. If your second property is a city condo, the same rule applies — same math, different view.

The verdict: one property, one shield

Elena and Frank aren't selling. They love the cottage, the grandkids love the cottage, and Frank's campfire songs have no other audience. But they're doing the one thing their friend's question forced them to do: they're looking at the actual number instead of pretending it's zero.

Your house gets the shield. Your cottage gets the tax bill. That's the rule, and knowing it twenty years early is worth more than learning it at the closing table.
