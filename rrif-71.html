71 Is Coming for Your RRSP

October 2026

Nadia is fictional — their story is invented to make the math stick. Every number in this article is real 2026 data.

Nadia is 58. She works as a school division administrator in Saskatoon. For twenty-five years she did exactly what the pamphlets said: she put money into her RRSP most years, left it alone, and watched it grow. Her RRSP is now worth about $500,000, and she has always thought of it the same way — her money, sitting there, waiting until she decides she needs it.

Last month, Frank — her retired brother — said something at Sunday dinner that rewired her whole picture.

"Just so you know," he said, "you don't get to decide forever. At 71, the government starts deciding how much comes out."

Nadia laughed. Then she stopped laughing, because Frank wasn't joking. By December 31 of the year you turn 71, your RRSP has to change into something else — usually a RRIF (Registered Retirement Income Fund) or an annuity. And a RRIF comes with a rule Nadia had never heard of: a forced minimum withdrawal every single year, whether you need the money or not.

This article is the explanation she wishes someone had given her at 40. If you have an RRSP, 71 is coming for you too — and the math of what happens next should shape what you do before you get there.

What actually happens at 71

An RRSP is a tax shelter, not a retirement plan. Every dollar you put in went in untaxed, and every dollar of growth happened tax-free. The CRA has been patiently waiting this whole time, and 71 is when the waiting ends.

By the end of the calendar year you turn 71, you must do one of three things with your RRSP:

  1. Convert it to a RRIF — what almost everyone does. Same investments, new account type, new rules.
  2. Buy an annuity — a lump payment to an insurance company in exchange for a fixed income for life.
  3. Take it all out as cash — one giant withdrawal, taxed all at once. Nobody does this on purpose.

The RRIF is the default choice. Your investments stay where they are. But the RRIF has a feature the RRSP never had: every year, a formula tells you the minimum you must take out. Not "may." Must. The money lands in your bank account, and it gets added straight onto your taxable income for that year.

Think of it this way: an RRSP is a jar you can leave closed as long as you like. A RRIF is a jar with a slow leak the government controls. You can always take out more than the minimum. You can never take out less.

The plain table: what the minimums actually are

The CRA sets a "prescribed factor" for each age — a percentage of your RRIF's value on January 1 that you must withdraw during the year. Here are the first five:

Age Minimum withdrawal
71 5.28%
72 5.40%
73 5.53%
74 5.67%
75 5.82%

Two things to notice. First, the percentages only go up — they keep climbing with age (past 94, the factor caps at 20%). The RRIF is designed to drain. Second, the percentage applies to whatever the account is worth each January 1, so in a good market year, the forced withdrawal in dollars gets bigger.

The worked example: Nadia's $500,000

Nadia's RRSP is worth $500,000. Say she converts it to a RRIF at 71. On January 1 of that year, the factor is 5.28%:

$500,000 × 5.28% = $26,400

That $26,400 must come out of the RRIF during the year. It does not matter that Nadia is still working part-time. It does not matter that she doesn't need the money. It does not matter that she'd rather leave it growing. $26,400 comes out, and it is added to her taxable income for that year — stacked on top of her salary, her CPP, and anything else she earns.

And here is the part Frank warned her about: that forced income can push her total income over the OAS clawback threshold, where the government starts taking back her Old Age Security payments — we explain that threshold in our OAS clawback article, but the key point is that money she never wanted to withdraw can shrink a benefit she was counting on.

The planning point: the years before 71 are your shaping years

Here is the sentence worth the whole article: the years before 71 are the last years you control which accounts hold your money. After 71, the RRIF formula is in charge. Before 71, you are.

Nadia is 58. She has thirteen years to shape things. In plain words, shaping means:

  • Draw down the RRSP early in low-income years. If there is a year where your income is unusually low — a sabbatical, a gap between jobs, the early years of semi-retirement — that is the cheapest year to pull money out of the RRSP, because the tax rate is the lowest. Every dollar you withdraw at a low rate is a dollar the RRIF can never force out at a high rate later.
  • Fill the TFSA. Money moved from an RRSP into a TFSA stops being the government's business. It grows tax-free and comes out tax-free — and TFSA withdrawals do not count as income, so they never trigger the OAS clawback. This is the single most powerful move for someone approaching 71 with a large RRSP.
  • Plan the withdrawal order. The decumulation order — which account you draw from first in retirement — decides how much tax you pay over your whole retirement. The general shape: spend down RRSP money in your 60s (when you may be in a lower bracket and can melt the balance gradually), keep the TFSA for later, and let the forced RRIF minimums be as small as possible when they start.

None of this is urgent at 58 in the panicky sense. But it is time-limited. Every year that passes is one fewer low-income year you can use to reshape the pile.

Great IF… / skip IF…

Deliberate RRSP drawdowns before 71 are great IF:

  • You will have low-income years before 71. Phased retirement, a planned gap year, a spouse's parental leave that drops household income — any year where your tax rate dips is a year to pull RRSP money out cheaply and move it to the TFSA.
  • Your RRSP is large enough that the forced minimums will hurt. If the RRIF minimum at 71-plus pushes your income into a higher bracket or into OAS clawback territory, shrinking the RRSP ahead of time pays for itself. Run the number: account value × 5.28% and up — is that more income than you want forced on you?

Skip IF:

  • You still need every dollar of RRSP room. If you are still in your high-earning years, still contributing, and still getting a big tax deduction on every dollar in — do not start pulling money out early just because 71 exists. The deduction you get now is worth more than the tax you'd save later. The shaping years matter most in your 60s, not your 40s.
  • Your RRSP is small. If the forced minimum at 71 is a few thousand dollars a year that won't change your tax bracket or touch your OAS, there is nothing to plan around. Spend your energy on growing the accounts, not draining them early.

The one thing Nadia is doing now

Nadia went home, opened a spreadsheet — Priya's influence — and ran the forced-withdrawal math on her own $500,000. Then she ran it on $400,000, the balance she would have if she moved $100,000 into her TFSA and non-registered accounts over her low-income early-retirement years. The forced withdrawal at 71 drops from $26,400 to $21,120. Same money, different accounts, less tax, less clawback risk.

She called it "the most useful Sunday-dinner conversation of my life." Frank, who spent years ignoring his own RRSP, just nodded. "Seventy-one comes for everyone," he said. "The ones who plan get to choose how."
