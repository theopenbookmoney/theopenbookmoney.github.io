Pay Off the Mortgage or Invest?

October 2026

Rob and Priya are inspired by real people — names and details changed so their stories can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real 2026 data or a clearly labelled illustration.

Rob and Priya have been in their Saskatoon house for four years now. Two incomes — night-shift supervisor, nurse — and two kids, Maya, 8, and Liam, 5. The mortgage is grinding along like it should.

And lately, something new has started happening: there's money left over at the end of the month.

Not a fortune. About $500. But it's real, and it keeps showing up, and now Rob and Priya are having the argument that every mortgaged couple in Canada eventually has.

Rob says: "Let's throw it at the mortgage. Get rid of this thing faster."

Priya says: "Let's invest it. We could earn more."

Both of them are right in a way. Both of them are wrong in a way. This is the one money question with no villain — just two good choices competing for the same dollars. So let's run the actual math and settle it.

The guaranteed return nobody talks about

Priya, the spreadsheet keeper, explained it to Rob over tea like this.

Every dollar you send to your mortgage earns a "return" equal to your mortgage rate. That's not a metaphor — it's math. If your mortgage charges 5% interest, then every extra dollar you pay down saves you 5 cents of interest a year, guaranteed, forever. No market crash can take it back.

That is the magic word here: guaranteed. Stocks can return 6%, 8%, 10% — or they can drop 20% the month before you check. Your mortgage interest savings? Locked in. The moment you overpay, that interest is gone for good.

The other side: investing usually earns more

Rob had his comeback ready. "But Priya — if we put that $500 in our TFSA and it grows at 6%, we come out ahead. The bank itself told us investments beat mortgage rates long-term."

He's right about the arithmetic. Investing usually beats a 5% mortgage over long stretches. The word "usually" is doing all the heavy lifting in that sentence.

The honest version: investing might earn more, but it's not guaranteed. The mortgage payoff IS guaranteed. So this whole debate is really one question wearing a costume: how much do you value a guaranteed 5% over a possible 6%?

The worked example: Rob and Priya's $500 a month

Priya ran both paths for ten years. Same $500 a month, same starting line.

Path A: $500 a month extra onto a $300,000 mortgage at 5%.

Every extra payment dodges 5% interest. After 10 years of $500 monthly overpayments, the total value of those payments — interest avoided plus the balance knocked down — comes to about $77,600. That's $60,000 of their own money, plus roughly $17,600 of interest they never have to pay. Guaranteed. No risk, no drama, no watching the markets.

Path B: $500 a month invested, earning 6% a year.

Here's where honesty matters: that 6% is an illustration, not a promise. Some decades markets earn far more; some years they earn far less. But IF the investments earned 6% — steady, average, no panics — after 10 years they'd hold about $81,900. That's the same $60,000 of their own money, plus roughly $21,900 of growth.

The scoreboard: investing wins by about $4,300 over ten years — IF the 6% shows up.

Read that again. The "risky" choice wins by four grand over a decade. Not forty. Four. That's the price of the guarantee: $4,300 of upside, traded for ten years of sleeping soundly.

One more thing the numbers don't show: it's also tax-free

Here's a bonus for Team Mortgage that's easy to miss. When your mortgage interest savings are guaranteed 5%, that 5% is after-tax — you don't pay a cent of tax on interest you never owed. Investment gains, depending on the account, can be taxed (in a TFSA they aren't — which is exactly why Priya would run this through her TFSA if they chose Path B).

It's a small detail, but it narrows the gap even further in the mortgage's favour.

The tiebreaker: the sleep test

The math says investing probably wins by a modest amount. So why would anyone choose the mortgage?

Because math doesn't know Rob. And this is where the article stops being about numbers and starts being about people.

Priya gave Rob a test: "Close your eyes. It's 2 a.m. Which choice lets you fall back asleep?"

If picturing your money in the market — dropping 15% in a bad year while you still owe the bank — keeps you up, pay the mortgage. Peace of mind is a return too, and it's the only one you get every single night.

If picturing a bigger mortgage balance while your investments compound keeps you up, invest.

Neither answer is wrong. A guaranteed 5% you can live with beats a 6% that has you panic-selling in year three.

The other tiebreaker: your job

There's one more question that breaks the tie fast: how safe is your income?

A mortgage overpayment is a one-way door — that money is trapped in your house until you sell or refinance. If Rob's warehouse shifts got cut, he can't pay for groceries with equity.

So if your job is shaky, or your industry is wobbling, or you're a contractor with lumpy income: the guaranteed win argues for the mortgage. Every extra dollar you pay down shrinks the balance you'd still owe if things went sideways. Safety first, growth later.

If both incomes are rock solid and your emergency fund is full (that's a whole other article, and yes, it comes first — money you might need in a hurry should never be locked in a house OR in the market): then the investing path earns its shot.

What Rob and Priya actually did

They split it. Rob's need for sleep won the first $300 — straight onto the mortgage, guaranteed. Priya's spreadsheet won the remaining $200 — into her TFSA, automated, untouched.

Priya's rule for the split: "The mortgage part is our insurance. The investing part is our ambition. We need both."

It isn't the mathematically perfect answer. It might be the human perfect answer.

The one-sentence version

Compare your mortgage rate to what investing might earn — the mortgage gives you a guaranteed return equal to your rate, investing might give you more but isn't guaranteed, so run the numbers, then pick the one that lets you sleep.
