New Car vs Used Car: The $10,000 Question

October 2026

Rob is inspired by a real person — names and details changed so the story can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real 2026 data or a clearly labelled assumption.

The minivan math

Rob's minivan is nine years old. It still runs. But the air conditioning died in July, the sliding door sticks, and Maya has started calling it "the embarrassing car."

Last Saturday Rob did the thing every car owner does eventually: he walked into a dealership "just to look."

The new minivan on the floor: $35,000. Shiny. Smells like plastic. Full warranty.

That night he found the same model, three years old, 60,000 kilometres, one owner: $22,000.

"Thirteen thousand dollars cheaper," Rob told Priya. "Same van. Is the new one really worth thirteen grand more?"

Priya opened the spreadsheet. "Wrong question," she said. "The sticker isn't the price. Ask me what each one costs over eight years."

This article is her answer.

The sticker-price illusion

Nobody pays the sticker price and stops. A car costs you four things:

  1. Depreciation — how much value it loses while you own it.
  2. Insurance — every year, no exceptions.
  3. Maintenance and repairs — oil, brakes, tires, and the surprises.
  4. Fuel — we'll assume the same model burns the same fuel, so this one ties.

Depreciation is the big one. It is also the invisible one. It never shows up on a bank statement. Your car just quietly becomes worth less every single month.

And here is the thing about depreciation: it is front-loaded. The first owner pays the most.

How fast new cars lose value

Canadian auto data tells a consistent story. A new car loses roughly 20 to 30% of its value in the first year. By year five, it has lost about 60%. After that, the bleeding slows down.

Picture a $35,000 car on a typical curve (labelled assumption — real cars vary by brand and condition):

Age Worth about
New $35,000
1 year $26,250
3 years $18,966
5 years $13,703
8 years $9,989

Read the first three rows again. In three years, the car lost $16,000 — almost half its value. Someone paid $35,000 and drove it for three years, and $16,000 vanished into thin air.

Now look at it from the other side. The person who buys that three-year-old car for $19,000–$22,000 never pays for those three years. The steepest, most painful part of the curve already happened — to someone else.

That is the entire used-car argument in one picture. You are not buying a worse car. You are buying the same car after the expensive years.

The worked example: eight years, two choices

Priya ran both options over eight years. Labelled assumptions throughout — swap in your own numbers and the verdict updates.

Option A: buy new for $35,000.

Cost over 8 years Math
Depreciation ($35,000 → ~$10,000) $25,000
Insurance ($1,800/year) $14,400
Maintenance (warranty covers the first 3 years) $5,600
Total 8-year cost $45,000

Option B: buy the 3-year-old for $22,000.

Cost over 8 years Math
Depreciation ($22,000 → ~$8,400 at age 11) $13,600
Insurance ($1,400/year — older cars cost 10–30% less to insure) $11,200
Maintenance (no warranty, older parts) $9,600
Total 8-year cost $34,400

The gap: about $10,600.

Notice what happened. The sticker said the difference was $13,000. The real eight-year difference is about $10,600 — smaller, because the older car needs more repairs and the new one carries a warranty. But it is still ten grand, and it comes almost entirely from one line: depreciation. The new car lost $25,000 of value. The used one lost $13,600. Everything else is noise next to that.

And the gap grows with the sticker. Run the same math on a $50,000 vehicle — close to today's average new price in Canada — and the eight-year gap pushes toward $17,000. The pricier the car, the more the first owner pays for the privilege of being first.

Buy new IF…

Scenario honesty, the way this site works. New is not dumb. It is right for some people.

Buy new IF:

  • You keep cars 10+ years. The longer you hold, the more the depreciation pain spreads out. A new car driven for 12 years is a perfectly rational purchase.
  • You value the warranty and the peace of mind. Three-plus years of "if it breaks, they fix it" is worth real money — especially if a surprise $2,000 repair would wreck your month.
  • You drive a lot. High kilometres burn through a used car's remaining life faster. Warranty coverage matters more when you drive 30,000 km a year.
  • You need the latest safety tech. If you're hauling kids every day, newer crash-avoidance features aren't vanity — though a 2–3-year-old car has most of them too.

Buy used IF…

Buy used IF:

  • You want the same transport for less. A 3-year-old car is the same car minus the steepest depreciation. This is the whole game.
  • You can handle some uncertainty. Older cars surprise you. Budget for it — Priya's rule is $1,200 a year in a "car surprises" fund — and the surprises stop being emergencies.
  • You get it inspected. A $150 pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic is the cheapest insurance in car buying. Walk away from any seller who says no.
  • You check the history. Accident records, service history, recalls. In Canada, a UVIP or Carfax-style report costs little and reveals a lot.

Skip the 6–10-year-old bargain IF you can't stomach repair bills. That is where the cheapest sticker prices live — and where the maintenance column in our table starts to bite. The sweet spot is 2–4 years old: modern features, warranty possibly still alive, steepest depreciation already paid by someone else.

The bottom line

Rob bought the three-year-old van. $22,000. Got the inspection — clean. Put the $13,000 he didn't spend toward the mortgage instead.

Six months later the sliding door sticks sometimes. Maya still calls it the embarrassing car. Rob calls it the $10,000 he kept.

The sticker price is not the price. Depreciation is the price — and the first owner always pays the most. Let someone else be first.
