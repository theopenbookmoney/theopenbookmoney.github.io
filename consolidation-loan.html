The Loan That 'Fixes' Your Debt Usually Makes It Worse

October 2026

Sam is inspired by a real person — names and details changed so the story can be told honestly. The debt example in this article is hypothetical, with every assumption labelled — it is not Sam's real debt.

Sam is 45, divorced, and rebuilding in a rented townhouse in Saskatoon. He works with Rob, and he has no tolerance left for financial fluff. The divorce split everything down the middle, and he started over with half a retirement fund and a determination to never be sold something dressed up as help.

So when the consolidation loan offer showed up — "One easy payment! Cut your monthly bill by more than half!" — Sam didn't feel relieved. He felt suspicious.

He brought it to Priya — Rob's wife, the household's spreadsheet keeper — and asked the only question that matters: "Is this actually a fix, or does it just feel like one?"

She ran the numbers. And the answer is why this article exists.

The pitch, stated plainly

A consolidation loan rolls your debts into one new loan. One payment instead of many. Usually a lower interest rate than a credit card. And almost always a lower monthly payment.

That last part is the whole sales pitch. A lower monthly payment feels like breathing room. When money is tight, breathing room feels like rescue.

But a lower monthly payment is not the same thing as less debt. Most of the time, it's the opposite: the payment drops because the loan stretches out over more years. And interest charges for time. More time in debt means more interest paid — even at a lower rate.

The reframe: two numbers matter, not one

Forget the monthly payment for a minute. Forget the interest rate, too, on its own. The two numbers that decide whether a consolidation loan helps you are:

  • Total interest paid — every dollar that goes to the lender instead of to your debt.
  • Months in debt — how long until you are actually free of it.

A loan that lowers your payment but raises either of those numbers is not a fix. It's a delay that charges interest.

The worked example: hypothetical, all assumptions labelled

Imagine a hypothetical person with one credit card balance. Not Sam's — a made-up example, so you can follow the math:

  • Balance: $8,000
  • Card interest rate: 21% per year, charged monthly
  • No fees, no new spending, every payment on time

Here are three ways to pay it off:

Monthly payment Months in debt Total interest Total paid
A: Keep the card, pay $500/month $500 19 $1,468 $9,468
B: Consolidation loan, 12%, 5 years $178 60 $2,677 $10,677
C: Same consolidation loan, keep paying $500/month $500 18 $762 $8,762

Read that table twice, because it holds the whole article.

Path B is the offer in the mailbox. The rate drops from 21% to 12% — nearly half! The payment drops from $500 to $178 — a $322-a-month raise, basically. It feels like a rescue.

And it costs about $1,200 more than just paying the card off, and keeps you in debt for 41 extra months. Five years instead of nineteen months. The lower rate lost to the longer term. Time beat the rate.

Path C is the same loan done right. Same 12% rate, same $8,000 — but instead of dropping the payment to $178, you keep paying $500. Now the loan wins properly: debt-free in 18 months, only $762 in interest, about $700 less than paying the card directly.

Same loan. Opposite results. The difference was never the loan. It was the payment.

When consolidation genuinely helps

This is not an anti-consolidation article. The editorial rule here is scenario honesty: consolidation helps clearly in the right scenario. It helps IF:

  • The rate drops AND the payoff stays as fast or faster. Both conditions, not one. A lower rate with a longer term is how Path B happens. Keep your payment the same — or raise it — and let the lower rate do its real work, like Path C.
  • The spending that built the debt stops. A consolidation loan pays off the cards. It does not fix whatever filled them. If the spending doesn't change, the loan is a pause, not a plan.
  • The fees don't eat the savings. Some loans charge origination fees or penalties. Read the fine print and add every fee to the "total interest" column before you sign. A cheap rate with a fat fee is an expensive loan.
  • Your house is not on the line. If the loan is secured against your home, you have just turned unsecured debt — the kind that cannot take your house — into debt that can. That trade is almost never worth a lower rate. An unsecured loan keeps the risk where it belongs.

The usual trap: two piles of debt

Here's the trap Path B sets, and it has nothing to do with interest rates.

The loan pays off your cards. Your cards are now empty. Empty cards feel like money. And for a lot of people, the freed-up credit quietly fills back up — a car repair here, Christmas there, a summer that got expensive. A year later, the picture is worse than where you started: the consolidation loan is still there, being paid off at $178 a month, and the cards have balances again.

Two piles of debt. One payment that felt like a fix, plus the old problem back on top of it.

The other version of the trap is softer and just as costly: the $322 a month of "relief" from Path B gets absorbed into everyday spending instead of going toward the debt. Nothing dramatic happens. Nobody runs up the cards. The loan just quietly takes five years and $2,677 in interest, because the breathing room never got pointed at the debt.

The verdict: a tool, not a fix

A consolidation loan is a tool. Like any tool, it helps when it makes the job cheaper and faster, and hurts when it just makes the job feel easier this month.

Before you sign one, run your own version of the table above. Write down your balance, your rate, and your payment. Then ask three questions:

  1. Is my total interest lower? Not my monthly payment — my total interest.
  2. Am I debt-free sooner? Not more comfortable — sooner.
  3. What stops the debt from coming back? A loan pays off cards. Only a spending change keeps them paid off.

If the answer to all three is yes, the loan is a genuine fix, and Path C is waiting. If any answer is no, the loan isn't fixing your debt. It's rescheduling it — and charging you for the delay.

Sam didn't take the offer. Not because consolidation loans are evil. Because the offer's payment was $178, and the offer's price was five years.
