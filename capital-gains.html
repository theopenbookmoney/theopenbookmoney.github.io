The Capital Gains Tax Hike That Never Happened

October 2026

Sam is fictional — his story is invented to make the math stick. Every number in this article is real 2026 data or a clearly labelled assumption.

The news Sam never checked

Sam has some investments outside his RRSP and TFSA. Stocks he bought years ago. They have grown. Lately he has been thinking about selling some.

Last month a buddy at work said, "Don't bother. The government jacked up the capital gains tax. You'll get killed."

Sam believed him. Everyone seemed to know about the hike. He saw headlines about it for months. So he did nothing. The investments sat there, waiting, while Sam waited for a tax change that had already been cancelled.

This article is for Sam, and for everyone who heard the same thing. The hike was real news. Then it died. Most people missed the funeral.

The myth, stated plainly

The myth goes like this: Canada raised the capital gains inclusion rate from 50% to 66.67%. If you sell investments for a big profit now, two-thirds of the gain gets taxed instead of half.

This is wrong. The rate is 50%. It never changed. Here is what actually happened.

What was proposed

In April 2024, the federal budget proposed a change. It was a real proposal, announced by the government, covered by every newspaper:

  • For individuals: the inclusion rate would rise from 50% to 66.67% — but only on the part of your yearly capital gains above $250,000.
  • For corporations and trusts: the rate would rise to 66.67% on all gains, no threshold.

The start date was supposed to be June 25, 2024.

That is why everyone remembers the hike. The headlines were loud. The proposal was real. But a proposal is not a law.

What actually happened: the timeline

April 2024 — the proposal. The budget announces the two-thirds rate. Headlines everywhere. People start planning around it.

January 31, 2025 — the delay. The Department of Finance pushes the start date back to January 1, 2026. The proposal still isn't law. The CRA had been treating it as if it might happen — which is normal for tax proposals — but Parliament never passed it.

March 21, 2025 — the cancellation. The Prime Minister announces the government is scrapping the increase entirely. Not delaying it again. Cancelling it. The two-thirds rate will never exist.

2026 — the reality. The inclusion rate is 50% for everyone. Individuals, corporations, trusts. Every dollar of gain. Section 38 of the Income Tax Act still reads one-half, and it never stopped reading one-half.

Read that again, because this is the whole article: the 66.67% rate never applied to anyone, for a single day. It was proposed, delayed, and killed. The law never changed.

Why everyone believes the hike happened

Nobody who believes this is foolish. The confusion was almost engineered:

  • The proposal got enormous coverage. "Capital gains tax hike" ran in headlines for a year.
  • The cancellation got a fraction of that coverage. Quiet Friday announcements don't go viral.
  • For months, the CRA was administering the proposal as if it might become law. Tax software asked questions about it. Accountants warned about it. It felt real because everyone was preparing for it.
  • Once something is in your head as "the new rule," you don't go back and check. Sam's buddy wasn't lying. He just stopped reading at the proposal.

This is how misinformation usually works. Not lies. Just the first half of the story, remembered, and the second half, missed.

What the rule actually is today

Here is the inclusion rate in plain words.

When you sell an investment for more than you paid, the profit is called a capital gain. Canada does not tax the whole gain. It adds half of it to your income. Then your normal tax rate applies to that half.

That is all "50% inclusion rate" means. Half counts. Half doesn't.

The worked example. Say Sam sells stocks for a $100,000 profit. (All numbers below use a labelled assumption: a 30% marginal tax rate. Yours will differ by province and income.)

Step Math
The gain $100,000
Included in income (50%) $50,000
Tax at 30% marginal rate $15,000

Sam keeps $85,000 of the $100,000 gain. The tax is not 30% of the gain — it is 30% of half the gain. That is the part people get wrong even when they know the rate. The inclusion rate is not your tax rate. It decides how much of the gain your tax rate touches.

The one thing that never changed: your home

Through all of this noise, one rule never moved and was never in danger: selling your principal residence is still tax-free.

The principal residence exemption wipes out the gain on your home before any inclusion rate touches it. It doesn't matter whether the rate is 50% or 66.67% — the exemption operates first, and the taxable gain is zero. You still have to report the sale to the CRA on Schedule 3, even when you owe nothing. But you owe nothing.

Anyone who told you "they're taxing your home sale now" was wrong twice: the hike never happened, and your home was never part of it.

The bottom line

Sam called his buddy back. "The hike got cancelled," he said. "March of last year." There was a long pause. "Huh," his buddy said. "I never heard that part."

Most people never heard that part. That is the entire lesson of this article, and it is bigger than capital gains:

Headlines announce proposals. Cancellations whisper. The tax code is full of things that were proposed loudly and killed quietly. Before you change your plans — before you hold an investment you wanted to sell, or rush to sell one you wanted to hold — check what the law actually says today, not what the headlines said last year.

The capital gains inclusion rate in Canada is 50%. It has been 50% for decades. A hike was proposed, delayed, and cancelled. The end.
