The Government Will Pay You $1,100 a Month If You're 'Poor Enough'

October 2026

Nadia is inspired by a real person — names and details changed so the story can be told honestly. Every number in this article is real October–December 2026 Government of Canada data, or a clearly labelled hypothetical example.

Nadia is 58, a school division administrator in Saskatoon, married, with a condo nearly paid off and RRSPs contributed most years. Retirement is seven years away, and she is doing the anxious math out loud: is it going to be enough?

So she started reading about retirement income. Old Age Security, CPP, RRIFs — the whole alphabet. And buried in the Old Age Security pages, she found a benefit she had never heard of in her life.

It's called the Guaranteed Income Supplement. And for a single senior with almost no income besides Old Age Security, it can add up to $1,138.90 a month — every month, tax-free, on top of the Old Age Security pension. That's the October–December 2026 figure, published by the Government of Canada. The title of this article rounds it to $1,100. The real number is higher.

Nadia isn't writing this for herself. By her own picture — steady jobs, RRSPs most years, a condo nearly paid off — she is not the senior this benefit was built for. She is writing it because of what she found next: a lot of the seniors who do qualify have never heard of it either. And unlike Nadia, they can't afford to miss it.

If you have a parent, an aunt, or a neighbour living mostly on Old Age Security, this is the article to forward to them. Better yet, sit down with them and check it together. That is the whole point of this piece.

The benefit, stated plainly

The Guaranteed Income Supplement — GIS for short — is a monthly, tax-free payment from the federal government. It sits on top of the Old Age Security (OAS) pension. It is not a separate pension and not a one-time cheque. It arrives in the same monthly deposit as OAS, for as long as the person keeps qualifying.

It exists for one reason: OAS alone is not much money. The maximum OAS pension in October–December 2026 is $762.50 a month for someone 65 to 74. A senior living on that and little else is living on about $9,150 a year. GIS is the top-up meant to close that gap.

Three things decide whether someone gets it, and how much:

  1. They must be 65 or older and living in Canada.
  2. They must already be receiving the OAS pension. Even a partial OAS pension counts. But if someone has delayed OAS — chosen to start it later for a bigger pension — they cannot get GIS until OAS starts.
  3. Their income must be low enough. This is where most people get confused, so let's do it carefully.

The numbers: what "poor enough" actually means

These are the maximums for October–December 2026, from the Government of Canada's payment tables. Amounts change every quarter — the next update comes in January 2027 — so check the current table before acting on them.

Situation Annual income must be less than Maximum GIS per month
Single, widowed, or divorced $23,112 up to $1,138.90
Couple, spouse also receives a full OAS pension $30,528 combined up to $685.56 per person
Couple, spouse receives the Allowance $42,768 combined up to $685.56 per person
Couple, spouse receives neither OAS nor the Allowance $55,392 combined up to $1,138.90

Read the first row twice. A single senior whose income is under $23,112 a year could receive up to $1,138.90 a month. That is up to $13,666.80 a year — money that does not need to be paid back, and is not taxed.

Two things people get wrong about that income line. First, your OAS pension itself does not count as income for this test. What counts is everything else: CPP, RRIF withdrawals, part-time earnings, a small workplace pension. Second, the maximum is a ceiling, not a promise. Most people who qualify get less than the maximum, because the payment shrinks as other income rises.

How income shrinks it

Service Canada describes the rule this way: for every $2 of other income, the maximum benefit drops by about $1. So the GIS fades out gradually. A little income costs you a little GIS. A lot of income costs you all of it.

Here is a hypothetical example, with every assumption labelled. Meet Margaret — not a real person, just arithmetic in a coat.

Margaret is 72, widowed, living alone in Saskatoon. She receives the full OAS pension, $762.50 a month, and a small CPP pension of $400 a month. She has no other income.

  • Her income for GIS purposes is just the CPP: $400 × 12 = $4,800 a year. (Her OAS does not count.)
  • Her GIS is reduced by about half of that: $4,800 ÷ 2 = $2,400 a year, or $200 a month.
  • Her GIS is therefore about $1,138.90 − $200 = $938.90 a month.

Add it up:

Per month
OAS pension $762.50
CPP pension $400.00
GIS $938.90
Total $2,101.40

Without GIS, Margaret would have $1,162.50 a month. With it, she has $2,101.40. That is the difference between scraping by and covering rent, groceries, and the bus pass with something left over. And remember, this is a hypothetical — your exact amount comes from Service Canada's estimator, not from this table.

How to apply (and how to keep it)

This is the part that trips people up, because it is two steps, not one.

Step one: get enrolled. In many cases Service Canada enrolls people automatically if it already has their information, and sends a letter saying so. If no letter ever arrived, do not assume there is nothing to claim — apply through Service Canada: online through a My Service Canada Account, by phone at 1-800-277-9914, or in person at a Service Canada office. If the senior in your life is not sure whether they are enrolled, the fastest check is a phone call with their Social Insurance Number handy.

Step two: file taxes every year, even with no income. This is the step that quietly ends more GIS payments than any other. Every July, Service Canada reviews each person's GIS using last year's tax return, and renews it, changes it, or stops it. No tax return, no renewal. The Government of Canada says it plainly: file by April 30 every year to avoid interruptions. A senior with no income at all still needs to file — a return showing zero income is the proof that keeps the payments coming. If taxes were missed, the person can also report their income directly to Service Canada to renew.

That second step is also the cruellest part of the design. The benefit built for people with the least paperwork depends on paperwork. Keep that in mind when you forward this article: the most helpful thing you can do for a parent may not be sending a link. It may be sitting beside them while they file.

Why so many eligible seniors miss it

This is not a guess. The government's own evaluation of the GIS looked at seniors who do not file taxes and found that two-thirds of those surveyed were likely eligible for GIS but were not receiving it. Among the barriers those seniors reported:

  • 47% could not easily find information online.
  • 39% thought their income was too high (many were wrong — remember, OAS itself does not count, and the single-person line is $23,112).
  • 29% did not manage their own finances.
  • 26% did not have photo ID.

Add the others the evaluation names — not knowing the benefit exists, not filing taxes, not understanding the application, isolation, mistrust of government — and a pattern appears. The people GIS was built for are the people least likely to be reached by a government website. They are reached by family. By neighbours. By a daughter who forwards an article and then picks up the phone.

That is why this article exists, and why Nadia — who will never receive a dollar of GIS herself — is the one telling you about it.

The verdict: check, then forward

The government will pay up to $1,138.90 a month to a single senior with almost no income besides Old Age Security. It is real, it is tax-free, and it is already budgeted for. The catch is not a trick — it is a form, a phone call, and a tax return every year.

So here is the checklist, for you or for someone you love:

  1. Are they 65 or older, living in Canada, and receiving OAS?
  2. Is their income besides OAS under the line for their situation — under $23,112 for a single senior?
  3. Did they ever get an enrolment letter? If not sure, call Service Canada at 1-800-277-9914.
  4. Did they file last year's taxes? If not, that is the first thing to fix.

If the answers point to yes, apply — even late. Applying late is still worth it; ask Service Canada about payments for past months when you call.

And if this article made you think of exactly one person, send it to them. That forward is the whole distribution system this benefit has.
